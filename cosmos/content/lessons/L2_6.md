## Limits of an expanding universe

In a static universe that has existed forever, we could eventually see every
galaxy. Our universe is different: it has a finite age and it expands. Light needs
time to travel, and space stretches while it does. This creates **horizons**:
boundaries that limit which parts of the universe can influence us.

Three distances are often confused. Let us take them one at a time.

## 1. The Hubble sphere

From the Hubble–Lemaître law $v = HD$, galaxies at the **Hubble distance**

$$D_{\mathrm{HS}} = \frac{c}{H}$$

recede at exactly the speed of light. The sphere with this radius is the
[[hubble-sphere|Hubble sphere]]. Today, with $H_0 = 67.7$ km/s/Mpc, its radius
is about **14.4 billion light-years** (4.4 Gpc).

:::warning The Hubble sphere is not a horizon
It is tempting to think that light from galaxies receding faster than light can
never reach us. Yet we routinely observe such galaxies. A galaxy at redshift
$z = 2$ is receding from us today at about **1.2 times the speed of light**. All
galaxies with redshifts above about 1.5 are beyond the Hubble sphere today.
:::

How is that possible? Imagine an ant walking along a rubber band towards you while
the band is being stretched. At first the stretching carries the ant away from
you faster than it walks. But if the stretching *rate* decreases, or the ant
reaches a part of the band that recedes more slowly, it eventually makes progress
and arrives. In the same way, the Hubble sphere of our universe has grown over
time, so light that was initially "losing ground" later found itself inside the
sphere and reached us.

## 2. The particle horizon: the edge of what we can see

The [[particle-horizon|particle horizon]] is the distance to the farthest objects
from which light could have reached us since the Big Bang. During each short time
interval $dt$ light covers a comoving distance $c\,dt/a$. Adding up all intervals
from the Big Bang until now:

$$\chi_{\mathrm{p}} = \int_0^{t_0} \frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = c\int_0^{1} \frac{da}{a^2 H(a)}$$

For the Planck 2018 universe this gives

$$\chi_{\mathrm{p}} \approx 14.2\ \mathrm{Gpc} \approx 46\ \mathrm{billion\ light\ years}$$

This is the radius of the [[observable-universe|observable universe]] today.

:::key 46, not 13.8
Light has travelled for 13.8 billion years, but the regions that emitted the
oldest light we receive have since been carried much farther away by the
expansion. The observable universe is therefore about 93 billion light-years across,
not 27.6.
:::

:::example Einstein–de Sitter
For a flat, matter-only universe, $a \propto t^{2/3}$ and the integral can be done
by hand: $\chi_{\mathrm{p}} = 2c/H_0 = 3ct_0$. The particle horizon is twice the
Hubble distance.
:::

The particle horizon grows with time, because light from ever more distant regions
has time to arrive. The observable universe gets bigger every year, by far more
than one light-year.

## 3. The event horizon: the edge of what we will ever see

The [[event-horizon|event horizon]] asks the opposite question: from how far away
can light emitted **today** ever reach us, no matter how long we wait?

$$\chi_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{t_0}^{\infty} \frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = c\int_{1}^{\infty} \frac{da}{a^2 H(a)}$$

- In a universe that decelerates forever (matter only), this integral is
  **infinite**: wait long enough and you receive light from anywhere. There is no
  event horizon.
- In a universe dominated by dark energy, $H$ approaches a constant and the
  integral is **finite**. Distant galaxies are carried away faster and faster, and
  their future light never catches up.

For our universe the event horizon today lies at about **16.6 billion light-years**
(5.1 Gpc). It will slowly approach $c/H_\Lambda \approx 17.4$ billion light-years
as dark energy takes over completely.

:::key Seeing the past, losing the future
Galaxies at redshift above about 1.8 are beyond our event horizon. We still see
the light they emitted billions of years ago, and we will keep seeing it, but
whatever they do from now on will never reach us. Over tens of billions of years,
distant galaxies appear to fade and freeze, their light redshifting towards
invisibility.
:::

:::try S2 Compare Planck 2018 with ΩΛ = 0. Only the accelerating universe has an event horizon.

## Putting it together: a spacetime diagram

The figure shows the three boundaries for our universe, as physical (proper)
distance against cosmic time.

{{figure:horizons_spacetime}}

- The **orange teardrop** is our **past light cone**: the positions at each moment
  of all the events whose light reaches us today. Strangely, it is widest about
  4 billion years after the Big Bang, at 5.8 billion light-years. Light we see from
  earlier times was emitted *closer* to us, because the universe was smaller.
- The light cone is widest exactly where it crosses the **Hubble sphere**. Inside
  the sphere, light moves towards us; outside, it is carried away.
- The **particle horizon** (blue) keeps growing: more of the universe becomes
  visible.
- The **event horizon** (red) and the Hubble sphere (green) approach the same
  constant size, because in the far future $H$ becomes constant.

| Particle horizon | 46.3 | Farthest material we can in principle see |
:::

## The horizon problem (a preview)

The particle horizon also existed in the past, and it was much smaller. When the
CMB was released ($z \approx 1090$), the particle horizon covered a patch of our
sky only about **two degrees** across. Regions of the CMB farther apart had never
been in causal contact, yet they have the same temperature to one part in
100 000. How did they "know" to be identical? This **horizon problem** is one of
the main motivations for cosmic inflation, an advanced topic of this course.

## Summary

- The **Hubble sphere** $c/H$ (14.4 Gly today) marks where recession reaches the
  speed of light. It is *not* a horizon: we see galaxies beyond it.
- The **particle horizon**, $c\int da/(a^2 H)$ from the Big Bang to today, is the
  radius of the observable universe: about 46 billion light-years.
- The **event horizon** exists only for accelerating universes; for ours it is
  about 16.6 billion light-years. Light emitted beyond it today will never reach us.
- Our past light cone has a teardrop shape, widest where it crosses the Hubble
  sphere.
