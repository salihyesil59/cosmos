## What is cosmology?

[[cosmology|Cosmology]] is the science of the universe *as a whole*: what it
contains, how it is arranged, how it began and how it changes over time.
Astronomy studies individual objects such as planets, stars and galaxies;
cosmology steps back and asks questions about the entire cosmos.

Before we can talk about the expansion of the universe or the Big Bang, we need a
feeling for **how big things are**. Cosmic sizes are so extreme that everyday
units and intuition break down. Our first tool is therefore a way of writing
numbers.

## Powers of ten

Instead of writing 1 000 000 we write $10^6$: a 1 followed by six zeros. Small
numbers use negative exponents: $10^{-3} = 0.001$. A number such as
150 000 000 000 metres becomes

$$1.5 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}$$

This is called **scientific notation**. Each step of one in the exponent is a
factor of ten, so $10^{11}$ is *ten times* bigger than $10^{10}$, not "a bit"
bigger.

:::tip Multiplying is adding exponents
To multiply powers of ten, add the exponents: $10^{3} \times 10^{8} = 10^{11}$.
To divide, subtract them: $10^{11} / 10^{3} = 10^{8}$. This makes rough
estimates of cosmic quantities quick and easy.
:::

## A ladder of sizes

The chart below shows some landmarks on a logarithmic scale, where every step to
the right is a factor of ten.

{{figure:scales_ladder}}

| Object | Approximate size | Power of ten |
|---|---|---|
| A human | 1.7 m | $10^{0}$ m |
| Earth (diameter) | 12 700 km | $10^{7}$ m |
| Sun (diameter) | 1.4 million km | $10^{9}$ m |
| Earth–Sun distance | 150 million km | $10^{11}$ m |
| Distance to the nearest star | 4.2 light-years | $10^{16}$ m |
| The [[milky-way|Milky Way]] galaxy | about 100 000 light-years | $10^{21}$ m |
| Distance to the Andromeda galaxy | 2.5 million light-years | $10^{22}$ m |
| Largest structures (superclusters) | hundreds of millions of light-years | $10^{24}$ m |
| The [[observable-universe|observable universe]] | about 93 billion light-years | $10^{27}$ m |

From a human to the observable universe spans **27 powers of ten**. If the
Sun were the size of a grain of sand, the nearest star would be another grain
about 30 km away, and the Milky Way would be as wide as the orbit of the Moon.

:::key Space is mostly empty
Stars are tiny compared with the distances between them. Galaxies, on the other
hand, are relatively close to each other compared with their sizes: Andromeda is
only about 25 Milky Way diameters away. This is why galaxies often collide, but
stars inside them almost never do.
:::

## Hierarchy of cosmic structure

The universe is organised in a hierarchy:

1. **Stars** like the Sun, often with planets.
2. **[[galaxy|Galaxies]]**, systems of up to hundreds of billions of stars, gas
   and dust bound together by gravity.
3. **Groups and clusters** of galaxies. The Milky Way belongs to the
   [[local-group|Local Group]] with Andromeda and dozens of smaller galaxies.
4. **[[supercluster|Superclusters]]** and the *cosmic web*: long filaments of
   galaxies surrounding huge, nearly empty voids.

On the very largest scales, above a few hundred million light-years, the
universe looks roughly the same everywhere. This observation will become a key
principle of cosmology in Level 1.

## Looking far means looking back

Light travels fast, about 300 000 km every second, but not infinitely fast.
Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach us, so we see the Sun as it was
8 minutes ago. We see the Andromeda galaxy as it was 2.5 million years ago.

:::key A cosmic time machine
The farther away we look, the further back in time we see. By observing very
distant galaxies, astronomers can directly watch the universe as it was billions
of years ago. This is one of the most powerful tools in cosmology.
:::

## Summary

- Cosmology studies the universe as a whole.
- Powers of ten make enormous and tiny numbers manageable; each step is a factor
  of ten.
- Sizes range over 27 powers of ten from a human to the observable universe.
- Matter is organised from stars to galaxies, clusters and the cosmic web.
- Because light takes time to travel, looking far away means looking into the
  past.
