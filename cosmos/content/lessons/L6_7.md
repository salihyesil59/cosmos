## Why look for alternatives?

ΛCDM needs two ingredients we have never detected in a laboratory: cold dark matter and
dark energy. Together they make up 95% of the universe. It is healthy, and scientifically
necessary, to ask whether the evidence could be explained differently. There are two broad
strategies:

1. **Change the ingredients**: keep general relativity, but replace Λ by something else.
2. **Change the law**: keep only the matter we see, but modify gravity.

## MOND: Modified Newtonian Dynamics

In 1983 **Mordehai Milgrom** proposed that Newton's law changes at extremely small
accelerations. Below a new constant of nature

$$a_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$$

the true gravitational acceleration $g$ becomes larger than the Newtonian value $g_N$:

$$g \approx g_N \quad (g_N \gg a_0), \qquad g \approx \sqrt{g_N\,a_0} \quad (g_N \ll a_0)$$

This is [[mond|MOND]]. Far from a galaxy's centre, $g_N = GM/r^2$ and circular orbits
have $v^2/r = \sqrt{GMa_0}/r$, so

$$v^4 = G\,M\,a_0$$

:::key MOND's successes
- **Flat rotation curves** follow automatically, without any dark matter halo.
- The **baryonic Tully–Fisher relation**, $v^4 \propto M_{\mathrm{baryons}}$, is observed to be
  remarkably tight across galaxies of all sizes, exactly as MOND predicts.
- With the single constant $a_0$ MOND fits the rotation curves of many individual galaxies
  from their visible stars and gas alone.
:::

:::warning MOND's problems
- **Galaxy clusters:** even with MOND, clusters need about twice as much mass as the gas and
  galaxies we see.
- **The Bullet Cluster:** lensing shows mass separated from the ordinary gas (lesson
  [L3.2](lesson:L3.2)), which is hard to reproduce without unseen matter.
- **The CMB:** the height of the third acoustic peak requires matter that does not interact
  with photons (lesson [L5.1](lesson:L5.1)). Relativistic versions of MOND must add extra
  fields that behave much like dark matter.
- **Structure formation:** without dark matter, baryon fluctuations cannot grow from 10⁻⁵ to
  today's structures in time (lesson [L5.3](lesson:L5.3)).
:::

MOND is therefore considered successful as a description of galaxies but incomplete as a
cosmology. Why galaxies follow its simple rule so closely is still an interesting open
question for dark matter models.

## Modifying general relativity

Another idea is to change gravity on the **largest** scales so that the universe accelerates
without dark energy. Examples include:

- **f(R) gravity**, which replaces the curvature term in Einstein's equations by a function
  of it;
- **scalar–tensor theories** (such as the Horndeski family), which add a new field coupled
  to gravity;
- **massive gravity** and **braneworld models** (e.g. DGP), in which gravity weakens at large
  distances.

These theories must reproduce general relativity precisely in the Solar System, where it is
tested to extraordinary accuracy. Many achieve this with *screening mechanisms* that hide the
modification in dense environments.

:::history A single event pruned the field
Many modified-gravity theories predicted that gravitational waves travel at a speed different
from light on cosmological scales. The neutron star merger GW170817 (lesson
[L6.5](lesson:L6.5)) showed the two speeds agree to one part in $10^{15}$, ruling out large
classes of these models within days of the announcement in 2017.
:::

Surveys now test gravity by comparing two things general relativity links tightly: the
**expansion history** $H(z)$ and the **growth of structure** measured through redshift-space
distortions and weak lensing. A mismatch would be a signature of modified gravity.

## Quintessence: dark energy that evolves

If dark energy is not a constant, the simplest alternative is **quintessence**: a slowly
rolling scalar field, similar to the inflaton (lesson [L6.3](lesson:L6.3)) but active today.
Proposed by Bharat Ratra and Jim Peebles and by Christof Wetterich in 1988, it has an
equation of state

$$w = \frac{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^2 - V(\phi)}{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^2 + V(\phi)} \geq -1$$

- **Thawing** models: the field was frozen with $w \approx -1$ and has recently started to roll,
  so $w$ rises above −1 today.
- **Freezing** models: the field rolled in the past and is slowing down towards $w = -1$.

Observations are usually summarised with the parameterisation
$w(a) = w_0 + w_a(1 - a)$ (lesson [L6.2](lesson:L6.2)).

### Phantom energy and the Big Rip

If $w < -1$, dark energy is called **phantom**. Its density *increases* as space expands.
The expansion rate diverges in a finite time, pulling apart galaxy clusters, galaxies, planets
and finally atoms: the **Big Rip**. For $w = -1.5$ it would happen about 22 billion years from
now. Simple scalar fields cannot produce $w < -1$ without instabilities, so phantom behaviour
would be a sign of more exotic physics.

{{figure:possible_fates}}

The fate of the universe therefore depends on the nature of dark energy: eternal acceleration
for a cosmological constant, a Big Rip for phantom energy, and even a Big Crunch if dark energy
were to fade away or turn negative.

:::key What the data say
Supernovae, BAO and the CMB together find $w$ within about 5% of −1 if it is constant. In 2024
and 2025 the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), combined with the CMB and supernovae,
found a preference, at the level of roughly three to four standard deviations depending on
the supernova sample, for evolving dark energy with $w_0 \approx -0.75$ and $w_a \approx -0.9$.
Dark energy would then have been stronger in the past and weakening today. This is one of the
most closely watched results in cosmology; Euclid, Rubin and further DESI data will test it.
:::

## How to choose between models

Cosmologists compare models using criteria like these:

1. **Fit to all data at once**, not just one observation.
2. **Simplicity**: extra parameters must improve the fit enough to be justified (formally via
   Bayesian evidence or information criteria).
3. **Predictions**: a good model predicts new observations before they are made, as ΛCDM
   predicted the BAO scale and the CMB polarisation.
4. **Physical consistency**: no instabilities, no conflict with laboratory or Solar System
   tests.

By these criteria ΛCDM remains the standard model, but the door is open: the Hubble and S8
tensions (lesson [L6.6](lesson:L6.6)) and the DESI hints are precisely where alternatives are
being tested.

## Summary

- MOND explains flat rotation curves and the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation with one constant
  $a_0$, but struggles with clusters, the Bullet Cluster and the CMB.
- Modified gravity theories try to explain acceleration without dark energy; GW170817 ruled out
  many of them.
- Quintessence is an evolving scalar-field dark energy with $w \geq -1$; phantom energy with
  $w < -1$ would end in a Big Rip.
- Data are consistent with $w \approx -1$, but recent DESI results hint at evolving dark energy.
- Models are judged by their fit to all data, simplicity, predictive power and consistency.
