## A modest assumption

For centuries people placed the Earth, then the Sun, at the centre of the
universe. Each time, observations showed that our location is not special. The
[[cosmological-principle|cosmological principle]] takes this lesson to its
logical end:

:::key The cosmological principle
On sufficiently large scales, the universe is **homogeneous** and **isotropic**.
:::

- **[[homogeneity|Homogeneous]]** means the same at every place: an average cube
  of 1 Gpc looks like any other such cube, wherever you put it.
- **[[isotropy|Isotropic]]** means the same in every direction: whichever way you
  look, you see the same average properties.

"On sufficiently large scales" matters. On small scales the universe is very
lumpy: planets, stars, galaxies and voids. The principle is about averages over
regions hundreds of megaparsecs across, in the same way that a sandy beach looks
smooth from a helicopter even though it is made of individual grains.

## The evidence

1. **The cosmic microwave background.** The oldest light in the universe arrives
   from all directions with the same temperature, 2.7255 K, to about one part in
   100 000 (lesson [L4.4](lesson:L4.4)). This is extraordinary isotropy.
2. **Galaxy surveys.** Maps of millions of galaxies, such as the Sloan Digital
   Sky Survey, show a cosmic web of filaments and voids. Above a few hundred
   megaparsecs, the web looks statistically the same everywhere.
3. **Distant radio sources and X-ray background** are spread evenly over the sky.

:::note Isotropy everywhere implies homogeneity
If the universe looks isotropic from *our* location, and we are not in a special
place (the Copernican principle), then it looks isotropic from every location.
A universe isotropic about every point must be homogeneous.
:::

## Expansion without a centre

The Hubble–Lemaître law $v = H_0 d$ is the only way a homogeneous and isotropic
universe can expand. Picture galaxies on a stretching grid. Seen from galaxy A,
galaxy B at distance $d$ moves away at $H_0 d$. Now move to galaxy B: the grid
stretches the same way, so B sees A (and everything else) receding according to
the same law.

:::warning The Big Bang was not an explosion in space
It is tempting to picture the Big Bang as a bomb exploding at a point and
galaxies flying outward into empty space. That picture has a centre and an edge,
and it is wrong. Instead, space itself expands everywhere at once, carrying
galaxies with it. There is no centre and no outside.
:::

:::try S7 Click different galaxies to stand on them. Each one sees all others receding in the same pattern.

## Why the principle is so powerful

Without the cosmological principle, describing the universe would require
knowing the density, pressure and motion at every point: an impossible task.
With it, the entire universe at any moment is described by just a few numbers,
such as its average density and its expansion rate. Mathematically, the geometry
of the whole universe reduces to one function of time, the **scale factor**,
which you will meet in the next level.

## Summary

- The cosmological principle: on large scales the universe is homogeneous (same
  everywhere) and isotropic (same in all directions).
- It is supported by the CMB and by galaxy surveys.
- Uniform expansion obeys $v = H_0 d$ for every observer; there is no centre.
- The principle reduces cosmology to a few numbers that change with time.
