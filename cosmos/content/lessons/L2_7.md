## "How far?" has many answers

Suppose we see a galaxy at redshift $z = 1$. How far away is it?

- When the light left, the galaxy was relatively close.
- While the light travelled, space expanded.
- Today the galaxy is much farther away than when it emitted the light.

There is no single "distance" in an expanding universe. Instead, cosmologists
define several distances, each tied to a specific way of measuring. At small
redshift they all agree with Hubble's law, $d \approx cz/H_0$. At large redshift
they differ enormously.

## Comoving distance

The [[comoving-distance|comoving distance]] is the distance on the expanding grid,
equal to the proper distance **today** (lesson [L2.1](lesson:L2.1)). Light covers a
comoving distance $c\,dz/H(z)$ for each small step in redshift, so

$$D_{\mathrm{C}} = c\int_0^{z} \frac{dz'}{H(z')} = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_0^{z} \frac{dz'}{E(z')}$$

with $E(z) = H(z)/H_0$ from lesson [L2.4](lesson:L2.4). The proper distance **at the
time of emission** was smaller by the scale factor: $D_{\mathrm{C}}/(1+z)$.

:::note Curved space
In curved space, the distance that matters for sizes and brightnesses is the
[[transverse-comoving-distance|transverse comoving distance]] $D_{\mathrm{M}}$. For
flat space $D_{\mathrm{M}} = D_{\mathrm{C}}$. For open space
$D_{\mathrm{M}} = \frac{c}{H_0\sqrt{\Omega_k}}\sinh\left(\sqrt{\Omega_k}\,H_0 D_{\mathrm{C}}/c\right)$,
and for closed space the sine replaces the hyperbolic sine (lesson
[L2.5](lesson:L2.5)). Our universe is flat, so we will not distinguish them below.
:::

## Light-travel distance

The [[light-travel-distance|light-travel distance]] is simply the speed of light
multiplied by the [[lookback-time|lookback time]]:

$$D_{\mathrm{LT}} = c\,t_{\mathrm{lb}}$$

It is what news reports usually mean by "a galaxy 13 billion light-years away". It
is useful as a statement about **time** (the light is 13 billion years old), but it
is not the distance to the object at any single moment.

## Luminosity distance

Astronomers often measure distances with standard candles: compare the known
luminosity $L$ with the observed flux $F$. The
[[luminosity-distance|luminosity distance]] is *defined* so that the familiar
inverse-square law still holds:

$$F = \frac{L}{4\pi D_{\mathrm{L}}^2}$$

In an expanding universe it is

$$D_{\mathrm{L}} = (1+z)\,D_{\mathrm{M}}$$

:::key Two extra factors of (1 + z)
Three effects dim a distant source:

1. The light spreads over a sphere of area $4\pi D_{\mathrm{M}}^2$.
2. Each photon loses energy: its wavelength is stretched by $1+z$.
3. Photons arrive less often: time intervals are stretched by $1+z$ as well.

Together the flux falls by $(1+z)^2$ more than the geometry alone suggests, which
is the same as increasing the distance by $1+z$.
:::

The **distance modulus** $\mu = m - M = 5\log_{10}(D_{\mathrm{L}}/10\,\mathrm{pc})$
used with supernovae (lesson [L3.3](lesson:L3.3)) is based on $D_{\mathrm{L}}$.

## Angular diameter distance

Another way to find a distance is to compare an object's true size $\ell$ with the
angle $\theta$ it covers on the sky. The
[[angular-diameter-distance|angular diameter distance]] is defined so that

$$\theta = \frac{\ell}{D_{\mathrm{A}}}, \qquad D_{\mathrm{A}} = \frac{D_{\mathrm{M}}}{1+z}$$

It is the proper distance of the object **when the light left it**, in a flat
universe.

Something remarkable follows. $D_{\mathrm{A}}$ grows with redshift at first, reaches
a maximum of about 5.9 billion light-years at $z \approx 1.6$, and then **decreases**.

{{figure:angular_size}}

:::warning Farther galaxies can look bigger
Beyond $z \approx 1.6$, a galaxy of fixed size appears **larger** on the sky the
farther away it is. The reason: we see it as it was when the universe was much
smaller, so it was physically close to us when it emitted the light we receive.
The CMB's last scattering surface, the most distant thing we see, has an angular
diameter distance of only about 40 million light-years.
:::

## Distances at a glance

Planck 2018 values, in billions of light-years:

| Redshift | Comoving (today) | At emission | Light-travel | Luminosity | Angular diameter |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 1.41 | 1.28 | 1.35 | 1.55 | 1.28 |
| 0.5 | 6.35 | 4.23 | 5.20 | 9.53 | 4.23 |
| 1 | 11.1 | 5.54 | 7.94 | 22.2 | 5.54 |
| 3 | 21.2 | 5.31 | 11.7 | 85.0 | 5.31 |
| 1090 (CMB) | 45.4 | 0.04 | 13.8 | 49 500 | 0.04 |

:::tip Which distance should I use?
- Brightness, standard candles, supernovae: **luminosity distance**.
- Sizes, angles, standard rulers such as BAO and CMB spots: **angular diameter
  distance**.
- "Where is it now?", counting galaxies in volumes: **comoving distance**.
- "How old is the light?": **lookback time** (or light-travel distance).
:::

## Two useful relations

The luminosity and angular diameter distances are tied together by the
[[etherington-relation|Etherington relation]], valid in any expanding universe
where photons are conserved:

$$D_{\mathrm{L}} = (1+z)^2\,D_{\mathrm{A}}$$

A consequence is **surface brightness dimming**: the brightness per unit area on the
sky of a galaxy falls as $(1+z)^{-4}$, independent of cosmology. Observations of
this *Tolman test* agree with an expanding universe, once the evolution of
galaxies is taken into account, and rule out simple "tired light" models of a
static universe.

:::try S1 Choose any redshift and check that the luminosity distance equals (1 + z)² times the angular diameter distance. Then find where the angular diameter distance is largest.

## Summary

- At low redshift all distances agree with $d \approx cz/H_0$; at high redshift
  they differ greatly.
- **Comoving distance** $c\int dz/H$ is the distance today; dividing by $1+z$ gives
  the distance at emission.
- **Light-travel distance** $c\,t_{\mathrm{lb}}$ describes the age of the light, not
  a position.
- **Luminosity distance** $D_{\mathrm{L}} = (1+z)D_{\mathrm{M}}$ includes redshift
  and time-dilation dimming.
- **Angular diameter distance** $D_{\mathrm{A}} = D_{\mathrm{M}}/(1+z)$ peaks near
  $z \approx 1.6$, so very distant objects look larger again.
