## Ripples of one part in 100 000

The [[cmb|cosmic microwave background]] has the same temperature, 2.7255 K, in
every direction, but not exactly. After removing the dipole caused by our own
motion (lesson [L4.4](lesson:L4.4)), tiny differences remain: typically about
**100 microkelvin**, or one part in 30 000.

{{figure:cmb_sky_patch}}

:::try S24 The CMB Sky Viewer shows the real WMAP nine-year map. Apply the analysis mask, then read the rms and the ratio to 2.7255 K: this is the sky the rest of the lesson is about.

These [[cmb-anisotropies|anisotropies]] are the imprint of small density variations
in the universe 380 000 years after the Big Bang. The same variations later grew
into galaxies and the cosmic web. Understanding their pattern turned cosmology into
a precision science.

## From a map to a power spectrum

A map of random-looking spots is hard to compare with theory spot by spot. What
matters is *how strong the fluctuations are on each angular scale*. Cosmologists
decompose the sky into **spherical harmonics**, the equivalent of musical notes
on a sphere. Each has a [[multipole|multipole]] number $\ell$ that corresponds to
an angular size of roughly

$$\theta \approx \frac{180^\circ}{\ell}$$

- $\ell = 2$ (the quadrupole) describes patterns spanning the whole sky;
- $\ell = 200$ describes spots about one degree across, twice the size of the full
  Moon;
- $\ell = 2000$ describes details of a few arcminutes.

The [[angular-power-spectrum|angular power spectrum]] $C_\ell$ gives the variance
of the fluctuations at each multipole. It is usually plotted as

$$D_\ell = \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2\pi}\,C_\ell$$

in units of μK², which gives roughly equal weight to all scales.

{{figure:cmb_power_spectrum}}

The spectrum has three regions: a flat **plateau** on large scales, a series of
**acoustic peaks**, and a **damping tail** on small scales.

## Large scales: the Sachs–Wolfe plateau

When the CMB was released, regions larger than about one degree on today's sky were
bigger than the distance light had travelled since the Big Bang (lesson
[L2.6](lesson:L2.6)). No physical process had time to change them. We see their
original fluctuations.

Photons climbing out of a denser region, which is a gravitational potential well,
lose energy and look cooler; photons from underdense regions look hotter. This is
the **Sachs–Wolfe effect**, discovered in 1967:

$$\frac{\Delta T}{T} = \frac{\Phi}{3c^2}$$

where $\Phi$ is the gravitational potential. If the initial fluctuations have the
same strength on all scales, as inflation predicts ($n_s \approx 1$), this part of
the spectrum is flat.

## Intermediate scales: sound waves

Before recombination, photons, electrons and nuclei formed a single hot
**plasma**. Gravity pulled the plasma into denser regions, while the enormous
photon pressure pushed back. The result was oscillation: **sound waves**, just like
air being compressed and expanded.

The waves travelled at roughly half the speed of light (58% early on, about 45% by
recombination as the baryons grew heavier relative to the photons),

$$c_s = \frac{c}{\sqrt{3(1+R)}}, \qquad R = \frac{3\rho_b}{4\rho_\gamma}$$

where $R$ measures how much the baryons weigh down the plasma. By recombination a
sound wave could have travelled the **sound horizon** $r_s \approx 144$ Mpc
(comoving).

:::key Snapshots of oscillations
At recombination every Fourier mode of the plasma was caught at a particular
moment of its oscillation. Modes that had just reached **maximum compression**
(hot) or **maximum rarefaction** (cool) produce the strongest temperature
differences: these are the **acoustic peaks**. Modes caught in between, at zero
displacement, produce the troughs.
:::

The first peak corresponds to waves that had time to compress exactly once, the
second to waves that compressed and then rarefied, the third to waves that
compressed twice, and so on. The peaks are therefore evenly spaced in $\ell$:

$$\ell_n \approx n\,\ell_A, \qquad \ell_A = \pi\,\frac{D_M(z_*)}{r_s} \approx 300$$

with a small phase shift that moves the first peak to $\ell \approx 220$.

## What the peaks measure

**The position of the first peak: geometry.** The sound horizon $r_s$ is a ruler of
known length at a known distance. Its angular size therefore measures the
curvature of space (lesson [L2.5](lesson:L2.5)). In a closed universe the peaks move
to lower $\ell$ (larger spots); in an open universe to higher $\ell$. Planck measures
the angle to 0.03%:

$$\theta_* = \frac{r_s}{D_M} = 0.5965^\circ$$

**The heights of odd and even peaks: ordinary matter.** Baryons add weight to the
plasma. The oscillation is shifted towards compression, so compression peaks (1st,
3rd, 5th) grow relative to rarefaction peaks (2nd, 4th). The ratio of the first two
peaks measures $\Omega_b h^2 = 0.0224$, in perfect agreement with Big Bang
nucleosynthesis (lesson [L4.3](lesson:L4.3)).

**The overall height: dark matter.** When modes entered the horizon during radiation
domination, the decaying gravitational potentials gave the oscillations an extra
push (*radiation driving*). More dark matter means earlier matter domination and
less driving. The third peak is especially sensitive, and its height requires
$\Omega_c h^2 \approx 0.12$: dark matter must exist and cannot be made of baryons.

**The slope: the initial fluctuations.** The spectral index $n_s = 0.965$ tilts
the spectrum slightly, showing that primordial fluctuations were a little stronger
on large scales, as inflation predicts.

## Small scales: damping

Photons were not perfectly locked to the electrons: they random-walked a short
distance between scatterings. This **diffusion damping** (or *Silk damping*, after
Joseph Silk) smeared out the smallest ripples. The spectrum falls exponentially
beyond $\ell \approx 1000$.

Later, when the first stars **reionised** the universe, about 5% of CMB photons
scattered again on free electrons, reducing all fluctuations smaller than the
horizon at that time by a factor $e^{-2\tau}$ with the optical depth
$\tau \approx 0.054$.

:::note Polarisation
Scattering also polarised the CMB slightly. Its E-mode pattern, measured by WMAP,
Planck, ACT and SPT, confirms the acoustic picture independently. A different
B-mode pattern would reveal gravitational waves from inflation; experiments such as
BICEP/Keck and the Simons Observatory are searching for it.
:::

## Six numbers from a curve

Fitting the full power spectrum with Boltzmann codes such as CAMB and CLASS gives
the six ΛCDM parameters of lesson [L3.4](lesson:L3.4) with percent precision. The
same model then predicts the distribution of galaxies billions of years later, which
is tested in the next lessons.

:::warning The model in this app
The Cosmos simulator uses a simplified, calibrated model. Peak positions follow the
real physics; heights are accurate to about 15%. It is ideal for understanding
trends, not for measuring parameters.
:::

## Summary

- CMB temperature fluctuations are about 100 μK; their strength per angular scale
  is the power spectrum $D_\ell$.
- Large scales show the flat Sachs–Wolfe plateau of the primordial fluctuations.
- Sound waves in the photon–baryon plasma, caught at recombination, produce acoustic
  peaks spaced by $\ell_A \approx 300$; the first sits at $\ell \approx 220$.
- Peak position measures curvature (space is flat); odd/even peak heights measure
  baryons; the third peak demands dark matter.
- Diffusion damps small scales; reionisation suppresses all but the largest scales
  by $e^{-2\tau}$.
