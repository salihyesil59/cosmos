## From linear ripples to bound objects

Linear theory (lesson [L5.3](lesson:L5.3)) works while density contrasts are small.
Eventually the densest regions reach $\delta \sim 1$ and something new happens:
they **detach from the expansion** and collapse under their own gravity.

## Spherical collapse

Picture a slightly overdense sphere in an Einstein–de Sitter universe. It behaves
like a small closed universe inside the larger one (lesson [L2.3](lesson:L2.3)):

1. At first it expands with the universe, but more slowly.
2. It reaches a maximum size (**turnaround**) and starts to fall back.
3. It collapses. In reality the matter does not reach a point: particles pass
   through the centre and exchange energy until the halo settles into
   **virial equilibrium**, where kinetic and gravitational energy balance.

Two famous numbers come out of this model:

- A region collapses when its **linear** density contrast, extrapolated with linear
  theory, reaches

$$\delta_c \approx 1.686$$

- The final halo is about **200 times denser** than the average universe (178 in the
  simplest model). This is why halo masses are often quoted as $M_{200}$, the mass
  inside a sphere 200 times denser than the critical density.

:::key A simple rule
Take the linear density field, smooth it on the scale of a halo, and look for regions
where it exceeds 1.686. Those regions have collapsed into halos of that mass. This
simple rule works remarkably well.
:::

## Bottom-up: small things first

How strongly the density fluctuates depends on the smoothing scale. Using the power
spectrum of lesson [L5.4](lesson:L5.4), the typical fluctuation $\sigma(M)$ is
**larger for smaller masses**. In cold dark matter:

- small halos reach $\delta_c$ first;
- they later **merge** into larger halos, which merge again;
- galaxy clusters, the largest objects, are still forming today.

This is **hierarchical** or **bottom-up** [[hierarchical-structure-formation|structure formation]].
The opposite "top-down" picture, where huge pancakes form first and fragment,
applies to **hot dark matter** such as fast neutrinos, which erase small-scale
fluctuations. Observations of galaxies at very high redshift and of the cosmic web
rule out hot dark matter as the main component.

:::example When do typical halos form?
For Planck 2018 parameters, typical (one-sigma) halos of $10^{8}\ M_\odot$ formed around
redshift 3, Milky Way–sized halos of $10^{12}\ M_\odot$ around redshift 0.4, while
cluster-sized halos of $10^{14}$–$10^{15}\ M_\odot$ are still rare peaks today. The first
stars formed in rare, early peaks of much smaller halos at redshift 20–30.
:::

## Counting halos: Press–Schechter

In 1974 **William Press** and **Paul Schechter** turned this idea into a formula for
the [[halo-mass-function|halo mass function]], the number density of halos of
each mass. With $\nu = \delta_c / \sigma(M, z)$:

$$\frac{dn}{d\ln M} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{\bar{\rho}_m}{M}\,\nu\,\left|\frac{d\ln\sigma}{d\ln M}\right|\,e^{-\nu^2/2}$$

The exponential makes massive halos extremely sensitive to $\sigma$: a small change
in $\sigma_8$ or $\Omega_m$ changes the number of massive clusters enormously. Counting
clusters is therefore a powerful cosmological test.

{{figure:halo_mass_function}}

:::note Refinements
Press–Schechter captures the physics but underpredicts massive halos by a factor of
a few. Later versions (Sheth–Tormen, Tinker) are calibrated on simulations and are
accurate to a few percent.
:::

:::try S27 Switch between Press–Schechter and Sheth–Tormen and compare them at 10¹⁵ M☉/h. Then raise σ8 by ten per cent and count the clusters on the sky.
:::

Haloes are only half the story: lesson [L5.8](lesson:L5.8) follows the gas that falls into
them and becomes galaxies.

## N-body simulations

Beyond simple models, cosmologists simulate the universe directly. An
[[n-body-simulation|N-body simulation]] represents dark matter by $N$ particles,
each weighing millions to billions of solar masses:

1. **Initial conditions:** particles are placed on a grid and displaced slightly
   using the linear power spectrum at high redshift (the Zel'dovich approximation).
2. **Gravity:** the force on every particle is computed, efficiently, with
   particle-mesh methods using fast Fourier transforms, or with tree codes.
3. **Time steps:** particles move and the force is recalculated, thousands of times.
4. **Analysis:** halos are identified as dense groups of particles.

{{figure:nbody_snapshots}}

:::history Milestones
- **1970s–1980s:** simulations with thousands of particles showed that cold dark
  matter produces a cosmic web resembling galaxy surveys (Davis, Efstathiou, Frenk and
  White).
- **1996:** Navarro, Frenk and White found that halos of all masses share a
  universal density profile, the NFW profile used in lesson [L3.2](lesson:L3.2).
- **2005:** the Millennium Simulation followed ten billion particles.
- **Today:** simulations such as IllustrisTNG, EAGLE and FLAMINGO also include gas,
  stars and black holes, and trillion-particle runs prepare for Euclid and the
  Rubin Observatory.
:::

## The cosmic web

Simulations explain the pattern seen by redshift surveys (lesson
[L1.4](lesson:L1.4)). Matter first flows out of **voids** onto flat **sheets**, then
along the sheets into **filaments**, and finally along filaments into **halos** at
their intersections. Galaxies form inside halos, where gas can cool and form stars.

:::try S13 Run the N-body simulator to D = 3. Watch sheets and filaments appear first, then small clumps that merge into bigger ones. Try warm dark matter with the same seed.

:::warning Small-scale puzzles
On galaxy scales, simulations with only dark matter predicted more satellite galaxies
and denser halo centres than observed ("missing satellites" and "cusp–core"
problems). Including the effects of supernovae, gas physics and faint-galaxy
discoveries has resolved much of this, but it is still debated whether dark matter
might be warm, self-interacting or fuzzy.
:::

## Summary

- Overdense regions detach from the expansion and collapse when their linear density
  contrast reaches $\delta_c \approx 1.686$, forming halos about 200 times denser than
  average.
- In cold dark matter, small halos form first and merge into larger ones: hierarchical,
  bottom-up growth.
- The Press–Schechter mass function predicts how many halos of each mass exist; massive
  clusters are exponentially rare and sensitive to cosmology.
- N-body simulations follow billions of particles and reproduce the cosmic web and the
  universal NFW halo profile.
