## Ghost particles

[[neutrino|Neutrinos]] are elementary particles with no electric charge and a tiny
mass. They feel only gravity and the **weak nuclear force**, so they hardly
interact with anything: about 60 billion neutrinos from the Sun pass through every
square centimetre of your body each second, and almost none of them is stopped.
They come in three **flavours**: electron, muon and tau neutrinos, each with its
antiparticle.

In the hot early universe, however, matter was so dense and hot that even
neutrinos were constantly created, scattered and absorbed. They were part of the
cosmic soup, sharing the same temperature as photons, electrons and positrons.

## Decoupling: when the weak force became too weak

A reaction keeps particles in thermal equilibrium only while it happens faster than
the universe expands (lesson [L4.2](lesson:L4.2)). For neutrinos the relevant
reactions, such as $e^+ + e^- \leftrightarrow \nu + \bar{\nu}$, have a rate that
drops steeply with temperature, while the expansion rate falls more slowly:

$$\Gamma_{\mathrm{weak}} \sim G_{\mathrm{F}}^2\,T^5, \qquad H \sim \frac{T^2}{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{\Gamma}{H} \sim \left(\frac{T}{1\ \mathrm{MeV}}\right)^3$$

Here $G_{\mathrm{F}}$ is the Fermi constant, which sets the strength of the weak
force, and $M_{\mathrm{Pl}}$ is the Planck mass.

{{figure:neutrino_decoupling}}

:::key One second after the Big Bang
The ratio $\Gamma/H$ drops below one at about **1–2 MeV** ($\sim 10^{10}$ K), roughly
one second after the Big Bang. From then on neutrinos
[[decoupling|decoupled]]: they stopped interacting and have streamed freely through
the universe ever since. This is the neutrino version of the photon decoupling
that produced the CMB 380 000 years later.
:::

After decoupling, the neutrinos kept their thermal energy distribution. Expansion
stretched their momenta just like photon wavelengths, so their temperature
continued to fall as $T_\nu \propto 1/a$.

## Why neutrinos are colder than photons

Shortly after neutrino decoupling, the temperature dropped below the rest energy of
electrons, $m_e c^2 = 0.511$ MeV. Electrons and positrons then
**annihilated** into photons: $e^+ + e^- \to \gamma + \gamma$.

The energy (more precisely, the entropy) of the annihilating pairs went into the
photons, which were still in equilibrium with the plasma. The neutrinos had already
decoupled, so they received nothing. The photons were heated relative to the
neutrinos.

Conservation of entropy gives the final temperature ratio exactly:

$$\frac{T_\nu}{T_\gamma} = \left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{1/3} \approx 0.714$$

:::example Where does 4/11 come from?
Before annihilation, photons (2 polarisation states) and electrons plus positrons
(4 states, each counting as 7/8 because they are fermions) shared the entropy:
$2 + \frac{7}{8}\times 4 = \frac{11}{2}$ units. Afterwards only the photons' 2 units
remain. The photon entropy grew by a factor $\frac{11}{2} / 2 = \frac{11}{4}$, and
since entropy scales as $T^3$, the photon temperature grew by
$(11/4)^{1/3}$ relative to the neutrinos.
:::

With today's CMB temperature of 2.7255 K, the neutrino background has

$$T_{\nu,0} = \left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{1/3} \times 2.7255\ \mathrm{K} \approx 1.95\ \mathrm{K}$$

:::try S1 At any redshift the Cosmology Calculator shows the CMB temperature. Multiply by 0.714 to get the neutrino background temperature at that time.

## The cosmic neutrino background today

The [[cosmic-neutrino-background|cosmic neutrino background]] (CνB) is as universal
as the CMB:

| | Photons (CMB) | Neutrinos (CνB) |
|---|---|---|
| Released | 380 000 years | 1 second |
| Temperature today | 2.73 K | 1.95 K |
| Number per cm³ | 411 | 336 (112 per flavour, including antineutrinos) |

Every cubic centimetre of space contains about 336 relic neutrinos. Yet their
energies are so low, about $10^{-4}$ eV, that no experiment has detected them
directly. Proposed experiments such as PTOLEMY aim to capture them on tritium, but
this remains extremely challenging.

## Measuring the neutrinos anyway

Even without direct detection, cosmology "sees" the relic neutrinos through their
gravity and their contribution to the expansion rate.

### The effective number of neutrino species

Neutrinos were part of the radiation that controlled the expansion of the early
universe. Cosmologists express their energy density with the
[[effective-neutrino-number|effective number of neutrino species]]
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$:

$$\rho_{\mathrm{r}} = \rho_\gamma\left[1 + \frac{7}{8}\left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{4/3} N_{\mathrm{eff}}\right]$$

For the three known neutrinos the Standard Model predicts
$N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.044$, slightly above 3 because decoupling was not perfectly
instantaneous and neutrinos received a small share of the annihilation energy.

- **Nucleosynthesis:** more radiation means faster expansion, earlier freeze-out of
  neutrons and more helium (lesson [L4.3](lesson:L4.3)). The observed helium
  abundance requires $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 3$.
- **The CMB:** Planck measures $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 2.99 \pm 0.17$, a clear detection
  of the neutrino background's effect on the early universe.

:::note Cosmos uses N_eff
The Planck 2018 preset in this app uses $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.046$ in the radiation
density, the standard value at the time of the analysis.
:::

### Neutrino masses

Neutrino oscillation experiments proved that neutrinos have mass: the sum of the
three masses is at least about 0.06 eV. Today the relic neutrinos are so cold that
at least two of the three types move slowly, behaving like a tiny amount of hot
dark matter. Their mass slightly suppresses the growth of galaxy clusters and the
cosmic web.

Cosmological observations therefore limit the total neutrino mass to roughly
**0.1 eV or less**, a tighter bound than any laboratory experiment. Pinning down this
mass is a major goal of current galaxy surveys.

## Summary

- Neutrinos interact only through the weak force and gravity.
- They decoupled when $\Gamma/H \approx 1$, at about 1 MeV, one second after the
  Big Bang.
- Electron–positron annihilation then heated the photons but not the neutrinos:
  $T_\nu/T_\gamma = (4/11)^{1/3}$, so the neutrino background is 1.95 K today.
- About 336 relic neutrinos fill every cubic centimetre, undetected directly but
  measured through $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 3$ in BBN and the CMB.
- Cosmology limits the sum of neutrino masses to about 0.1 eV.
