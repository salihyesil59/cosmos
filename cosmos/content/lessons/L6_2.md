## From geometry to dynamics

In lesson [L2.3](lesson:L2.3) we derived the Friedmann equation from Newtonian energy
conservation. The argument was elegant but left questions open. What exactly is $k$?
Why should pressure affect gravity? Does the derivation still hold for radiation or dark
energy?

General relativity answers all of these. The recipe has three steps:

1. Choose the metric: FLRW (lesson [L6.1](lesson:L6.1)).
2. Choose the matter: a **perfect fluid** with density $\rho$ and pressure $p$.
3. Insert both into Einstein's field equations.

We will not grind through every derivative, but we will follow the logic and check the
result.

## Step 1: the metric

For simplicity take flat space. In Cartesian comoving coordinates the FLRW metric is

$$ds^2 = -c^2\,dt^2 + a^2(t)\left(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2\right)$$

so the metric components are $g_{00} = -c^2$ and $g_{11} = g_{22} = g_{33} = a^2$, with
all others zero. Curvature adds terms proportional to $k$, which we will restore at the
end.

## Step 2: a perfect fluid

On large scales every ingredient of the universe behaves as a
[[perfect-fluid|perfect fluid]]: no viscosity and no heat flow, fully described by its
energy density $\rho c^2$ and pressure $p$. For observers moving with the fluid, its
energy–momentum tensor is simply

$$T^{\mu}{}_{\nu} = \mathrm{diag}\left(-\rho c^2,\; p,\; p,\; p\right)$$

The first entry is the energy density; the other three are the pressure pushing in each
space direction. By isotropy, the pressure is the same in all directions.

## Step 3: curvature of the FLRW metric

The Einstein tensor is built from second derivatives of the metric. For the FLRW metric,
all the derivatives involve only $a(t)$. The results are

$$G^{0}{}_{0} = -\frac{3}{c^2}\left(\frac{\dot{a}^2}{a^2} + \frac{kc^2}{a^2}\right)$$

$$G^{i}{}_{i} = -\frac{1}{c^2}\left(2\frac{\ddot{a}}{a} + \frac{\dot{a}^2}{a^2} + \frac{kc^2}{a^2}\right) \quad \mathrm{(no\ sum)}$$

All off-diagonal components vanish, a consequence of homogeneity and isotropy.

:::note Why only two equations?
Einstein's equations are ten equations in general. Symmetry reduces them to two
independent ones: the time–time part and the three identical space–space parts.
:::

## The time–time equation: Friedmann's first equation

Setting $G^{0}{}_{0} + \Lambda = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T^{0}{}_{0}$ and multiplying by
$-c^2/3$:

$$\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}$$

This is exactly the Friedmann equation of lesson [L2.3](lesson:L2.3), now with three
important clarifications:

- $\rho$ includes **all forms of energy** divided by $c^2$: rest mass, radiation, kinetic
  energy, and dark energy.
- $k$ is not a Newtonian energy but the **curvature of space** appearing in the metric.
- $\Lambda$ appears naturally as a term in the field equations.

## The space–space equation: acceleration

The space–space components give a second equation. Subtracting the first equation from it
eliminates $\dot{a}^2$ and $k$:

$$\frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\right) + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}$$

:::key Pressure gravitates
In Newtonian gravity only mass attracts. In general relativity pressure contributes to the
source of gravity with weight $3p/c^2$. For radiation ($p = \rho c^2/3$) this doubles its
gravitational pull. For a cosmological constant, whose pressure is $p = -\rho c^2$, the
combination $\rho + 3p/c^2$ is negative: gravity becomes **repulsive** and the expansion
accelerates. This is impossible to understand in Newtonian terms.
:::

## Energy conservation: the fluid equation

In general relativity the energy–momentum tensor obeys $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, a
consequence of the field equations themselves. For a perfect fluid in the FLRW metric this
gives

$$\dot{\rho} + 3\,\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\left(\rho + \frac{p}{c^2}\right) = 0$$

It is the first law of thermodynamics, $dE = -p\,dV$, for a comoving volume
$V \propto a^3$. For an equation of state $p = w\rho c^2$ (lesson [L3.1](lesson:L3.1))
it integrates to

$$\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}$$

:::example Check: differentiate the Friedmann equation
Differentiate the first Friedmann equation in time (with $\Lambda = 0$) and use the fluid
equation to replace $\dot{\rho}$. The $k$ terms cancel and you obtain precisely the
acceleration equation. The three equations are not independent: any two imply the third.
:::

## Evolving dark energy

If dark energy is not a constant but a fluid with a time-dependent equation of state, the
same machinery still works. A popular description is

$$w(a) = w_0 + w_a\,(1 - a)$$

Integrating the fluid equation gives
$\rho_{\mathrm{de}} \propto a^{-3(1+w_0+w_a)}\,e^{-3w_a(1-a)}$. Surveys such as DESI fit
$w_0$ and $w_a$ to test whether dark energy changes with time (lesson
[L6.7](lesson:L6.7)).

## What the Newtonian derivation got right, and wrong

| | Newtonian sphere | General relativity |
|---|---|---|
| Friedmann equation form | ✓ same | ✓ |
| Meaning of $k$ | energy of a test particle | curvature of space |
| Pressure as a source of gravity | missing | $\rho + 3p/c^2$ |
| Radiation and dark energy | only by analogy | included exactly |
| Cosmological constant | added by hand | natural term in the field equations |
| Light, redshift, horizons | not described | follow from the metric |

## Summary

- Inserting the FLRW metric and a perfect fluid into Einstein's equations leaves two
  independent equations.
- The time–time part gives $H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - kc^2/a^2 + \Lambda c^2/3$.
- The space–space part gives $\ddot{a}/a = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}(\rho + 3p/c^2) + \Lambda c^2/3$:
  pressure gravitates, and negative pressure causes acceleration.
- Energy–momentum conservation gives the fluid equation and $\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}$.
- General relativity turns the Newtonian analogy into an exact theory, with $k$ as genuine
  spatial curvature.
