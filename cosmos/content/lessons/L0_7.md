## Why bother, when there is a calculator?

Because the calculator will happily tell you the universe is 13.8 *million* years
old if you typed one wrong exponent, and only you can notice. Every working
physicist keeps a rough answer in mind *before* computing, and compares. Most errors
are caught that way, not by checking the algebra.

{{figure:orders_of_magnitude}}

The skill has a name — an **order-of-magnitude estimate**, or a *Fermi problem*,
after Enrico Fermi, who estimated the yield of the first atomic bomb by dropping
scraps of paper as the blast wave passed and watching how far they blew.

:::key The rule of the game
You are not trying to be right. You are trying to be right **to within a factor of
a few** — one power of ten at worst — using numbers you already know. That is almost
always enough to tell a sensible answer from a nonsensical one.
:::

## Powers of ten, by hand

Everything in cosmology is written as $a \times 10^{n}$. Three rules cover almost
all of it:

- **Multiply**: add the exponents. $(3\times10^{8})(2\times10^{-5}) = 6\times10^{3}$.
- **Divide**: subtract them. $(6\times10^{24}) / (3\times10^{8}) = 2\times10^{16}$.
- **Powers**: multiply the exponent. $(2\times10^{3})^{3} = 8\times10^{9}$.

The part that takes practice is the front number. Round it hard: $\pi \approx 3$,
$\sqrt{10}\approx3$, $365 \approx 400$, and a year is $3\times10^{7}$ seconds — a
number worth knowing, because $\pi\times10^{7}$ is accurate to half a percent.

:::tip Round in opposite directions
If you round one factor up, round the next one down. Errors that cancel keep you
inside your factor of a few; errors that pile up in the same direction do not.
:::

## Worked example: how many stars can you see?

A dark sky shows stars down to magnitude 6. How many are there?

The brightest star is magnitude $-1$; each step of 1 magnitude is a factor 2.5 in
brightness, and fainter stars are much more numerous. Rather than the luminosity
function, use a fact you already have: about 20 stars are brighter than magnitude 1,
and the count roughly triples per magnitude. Five magnitudes further down is
$3^{5} \approx 250$, so $20\times250 = 5\times10^{3}$ over the whole sky, half of
which is below the horizon.

The answer is **two to three thousand**. The real number is about 2 500. A
calculator was never involved.

## Units are a free check

An equation whose units do not match is wrong, full stop — and checking costs
seconds. If you half-remember the Friedmann equation as

$$H^2 = \frac{8\pi G\rho}{3},$$

check it: $G$ is m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², $\rho$ is kg m⁻³, so the right-hand side is s⁻². And
$H$ is a velocity over a distance, also s⁻¹, so $H^2$ is s⁻². It matches, and you
can use the formula.

Units also *reconstruct* formulas. What could a black hole's radius be, built from
$G$, $M$ and $c$? Only $GM/c^{2}$ has units of length. The real Schwarzschild radius
is $2GM/c^{2}$ — the factor of 2 needs the actual theory, but the shape of the
answer needed nothing but bookkeeping.

:::note The factor you cannot get
Dimensional analysis gives you the scaling and never the dimensionless number in
front. That number is almost always between 0.1 and 10, which is exactly why an
order-of-magnitude estimate is allowed to ignore it.
:::

## The numbers worth memorising

A small set carries most of cosmology. Learn these to one significant figure:

| Quantity | Value | Useful because |
|---|---|---|
| Speed of light $c$ | $3\times10^{8}$ m/s | everything |
| Year | $3\times10^{7}$ s | converts ages to seconds |
| Parsec | $3\times10^{16}$ m ≈ 3 light years | the distance unit |
| Hubble constant $H_0$ | 70 km/s/Mpc | expansion rate |
| Hubble time $1/H_0$ | 14 billion years | the age, near enough |
| Hubble distance $c/H_0$ | 4 300 Mpc | the size of the visible universe |
| Age of the universe | $13.8$ Gyr $= 4\times10^{17}$ s | the clock |
| CMB temperature | 2.7 K | the thermal history |
| Critical density | $10^{-26}$ kg/m³ | about 5 hydrogen atoms per m³ |
| Milky Way | $10^{11}$ stars, 30 kpc across | the local yardstick |
| Galaxies observable | a few $\times10^{11}$ | the census |

:::tip One number, two jobs
$c/H_0 \approx 4\,300$ Mpc and $1/H_0 \approx 14$ Gyr are the same fact in different
clothes. Knowing that the Hubble time is close to the actual age — and *why* it
needn't have been — is worth more than either number alone.
:::

## Worked example: the density of the universe

The critical density is $\rho_c = 3H_0^{2}/8\pi G$. Estimate it without a
calculator.

$H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc $= 7\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{m/s} \div 3\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{m}
\approx 2\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

Then $H_0^{2} \approx 4\times10^{-36}$, and $8\pi G \approx 25 \times
6.7\times10^{-11} \approx 1.7\times10^{-9}$. So

$$\rho_c \approx \frac{3 \times 4\times10^{-36}}{1.7\times10^{-9}} \approx 7\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}.$$

The accepted value is $8.5\times10^{-27}$ kg/m³. Divide by the mass of a hydrogen
atom, $1.7\times10^{-27}$ kg, and the universe contains about **five hydrogen atoms
per cubic metre** — emptier than the best vacuum ever made in a laboratory.

:::try S1 Check yourself against the machine
Estimate the age of the universe as $1/H_0$ in your head, then set the Cosmology
Calculator to z = 0 and compare. Why is the real age *close* to $1/H_0$ but not
equal to it?
:::

## The three ways it goes wrong

1. **A missed exponent.** By far the most common. Write every number as
   $a\times10^{n}$ and track the exponents separately from the front numbers.
2. **A missing factor that is not of order one.** Some numbers really are enormous —
   the ratio of electromagnetic to gravitational force between two protons is
   $10^{36}$. Estimating is fine; assuming every unknown factor is 1 is not.
3. **Estimating an exponential.** $e^{60}$ during inflation is not "a few". Where a
   quantity depends exponentially on something, small changes in that something
   change the answer by powers of ten, and rough is no longer good enough.

:::warning When an estimate is not enough
Precision cosmology measures parameters to a percent, and the entire Hubble tension
([L6.6](lesson:L6.6)) lives in a 9% disagreement. Order-of-magnitude thinking tells
you whether a measurement is *possible*; it can never settle a question like that.
Know which kind of question you are asking.
:::

## What to remember

- Estimate first, compute second, and compare. That habit catches most mistakes.
- A factor of a few is a success; one power of ten is usually still useful.
- Units check a formula for free and often reconstruct its shape.
- Memorise a dozen numbers to one significant figure — $c$, a year, a parsec, $H_0$,
  $1/H_0$, $T_\mathrm{CMB}$, $\rho_c$ — and most of cosmology becomes arithmetic.
- Watch out for exponents, for genuinely huge dimensionless numbers, and for
  anything exponential.
