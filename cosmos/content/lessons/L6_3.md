## Three puzzles of the hot Big Bang

The standard Big Bang model explains the expansion, the light elements and the CMB
beautifully. But it starts from very special initial conditions that it does not
explain.

### 1. The horizon problem

When the CMB was released, the particle horizon covered only about **two degrees** on
today's sky (lesson [L2.6](lesson:L2.6)). Regions further apart had never exchanged a
signal. Yet the CMB has the same temperature, 2.7255 K, in every direction to one part in
100 000. How could hundreds of causally disconnected patches agree so precisely?

### 2. The flatness problem

Deviations from flatness grow with time: $|\Omega - 1| \propto a$ in the matter era and
$\propto a^2$ in the radiation era (lesson [L2.4](lesson:L2.4)). Today
$|\Omega_k| < 0.002$, so at the Planck time $|\Omega - 1|$ must have been smaller than about
$10^{-60}$. Why was the universe tuned so precisely?

### 3. The monopole problem

Many grand unified theories predict heavy particles called magnetic monopoles, produced in
enormous numbers at energies near $10^{16}$ GeV. They would dominate the universe today. None
has ever been found.

## The idea: accelerated expansion

Between 1980 and 1982 **Alexei Starobinsky**, **Alan Guth**, **Andrei Linde**, and
**Andreas Albrecht** with **Paul Steinhardt** proposed that before the hot Big Bang, the universe
went through a phase of rapid, accelerated expansion: [[inflation|inflation]].

The key quantity is the **comoving Hubble radius** $c/(aH)$, the region, measured on the
expanding grid, within which causal processes can act at a given time.

- In a decelerating universe, $aH = \dot{a}$ decreases, so the comoving Hubble radius
  **grows**. More and more of the universe comes into causal contact.
- In an accelerating universe, $\dot{a}$ increases, so the comoving Hubble radius
  **shrinks**.

{{figure:inflation_hubble_radius}}

:::key How inflation solves the problems
- **Horizon:** before inflation the whole observable universe was inside one tiny causally
  connected patch. Inflation stretched it and pushed it outside the Hubble radius; it is
  now re-entering. The CMB is uniform because it all shared a common past.
- **Flatness:** during inflation $|\Omega - 1| \propto 1/(aH)^2$ shrinks dramatically. Any
  initial curvature is stretched flat, just as the Earth looks flat to us.
- **Monopoles:** any relics produced before inflation are diluted to fewer than one in the
  observable universe.
:::

How much inflation is needed? The comoving Hubble radius must shrink by a factor of about
$e^{60}$, i.e. the universe must expand by about 60 [[e-fold|e-folds]]:

$$N = \ln\frac{a_{\mathrm{end}}}{a_{\mathrm{start}}} \gtrsim 60$$

(the exact number depends on the energy scale and on how the universe reheated).

## Slow roll: a field on a hill

What could drive accelerated expansion? From lesson [L6.2](lesson:L6.2), we need a
substance with $p < -\rho c^2/3$. A **scalar field** $\phi$, the [[inflaton|inflaton]],
does the job. Its energy density and pressure are

$$\rho c^2 = \frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^2 + V(\phi), \qquad p = \frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^2 - V(\phi)$$

If the field changes slowly, so that the potential energy $V$ dominates the kinetic energy,
then $p \approx -\rho c^2$: almost a cosmological constant. The field obeys

$$\ddot{\phi} + 3H\dot{\phi} + V'(\phi) = 0$$

like a ball rolling down a hill with friction $3H\dot{\phi}$ from the expansion.

The shape of the hill is measured by the **slow-roll parameters** (in units of the reduced
Planck mass):

$$\epsilon = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{V'}{V}\right)^2, \qquad \eta = \frac{V''}{V}$$

Inflation lasts while $\epsilon \ll 1$ and $|\eta| \ll 1$, i.e. while the potential is flat
enough. It ends when $\epsilon$ reaches 1 and the field rolls quickly to the bottom.

:::note Reheating
At the bottom of the potential the field oscillates and decays into ordinary particles,
filling the universe with hot radiation. This **reheating** is the start of the hot Big Bang
described in Level 4. Inflation therefore does not replace the Big Bang; it sets its initial
conditions.
:::

## Quantum seeds of structure

The most remarkable prediction of inflation is quantitative. Quantum fluctuations of the
inflaton are stretched from microscopic to cosmic scales and freeze in when they leave the
Hubble radius. They become the density fluctuations that grow into galaxies (Level 5).

Slow-roll inflation predicts:

- **Gaussian**, **adiabatic** fluctuations (all components perturbed together), confirmed by
  Planck;
- a nearly **scale-invariant** spectrum with spectral index
  $$n_s = 1 - 6\epsilon + 2\eta$$
  slightly below 1, because the field rolls and $H$ changes slowly;
- **primordial gravitational waves** with tensor-to-scalar ratio
  $$r = 16\,\epsilon$$
- an amplitude set by the energy scale, $A_s = V/(24\pi^2 \epsilon\,M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^4)$.

:::key A test of inflation
Planck measures $n_s = 0.9649 \pm 0.0042$: significantly below 1, as slow-roll inflation
predicted decades earlier. Primordial gravitational waves have not yet been detected; the
BICEP/Keck and Planck data require $r < 0.036$ (95% confidence).
:::

{{figure:ns_r_plane}}

The data already exclude the simplest models:

- $V \propto \phi^2$ predicts $n_s \approx 0.964$ (fine) but $r \approx 0.14$ (too large);
- $V \propto \phi^4$ is excluded on both counts;
- **plateau models** such as Starobinsky's $R^2$ inflation or Higgs inflation predict
  $n_s \approx 0.965$ and $r \approx 0.004$, in excellent agreement.

:::example The energy scale of inflation
The tensor amplitude fixes the energy scale directly:
$V^{1/4} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{GeV} \times (r/0.036)^{1/4}$.
The current limit therefore says inflation happened below about $10^{16}$ GeV, a trillion
times the energy of the Large Hadron Collider. A detection of $r$ would measure it.
:::

## Searching for primordial gravitational waves

Gravitational waves from inflation would leave a unique swirling pattern in the
polarisation of the CMB, called **B-modes**. Experiments such as BICEP/Keck, the Simons
Observatory, SPT-3G and the planned LiteBIRD satellite aim to reach $r \sim 0.001$, enough
to test Starobinsky-like models.

:::warning Is inflation proven?
Inflation is the leading explanation of the initial conditions, and its predictions for
$n_s$, Gaussianity and adiabaticity have been confirmed. But there is no direct detection of
primordial gravitational waves, the inflaton has not been identified, and alternatives such
as bouncing or cyclic cosmologies are still studied. It is best described as a very
successful, but not yet confirmed, theory.
:::

## Summary

- The hot Big Bang needs unexplained initial conditions: the horizon, flatness and monopole
  problems.
- Inflation, an early phase of accelerated expansion lasting about 60 e-folds, shrinks the
  comoving Hubble radius and solves all three.
- A slowly rolling scalar field with a flat potential drives inflation; it ends when
  $\epsilon = 1$ and reheating starts the hot Big Bang.
- Quantum fluctuations predict $n_s = 1 - 6\epsilon + 2\eta$ and $r = 16\epsilon$.
- Planck's $n_s = 0.965$ and the limit $r < 0.036$ favour plateau models such as Starobinsky
  inflation; B-mode searches continue.
