## Gravity is unstable

A perfectly smooth universe would stay smooth forever. But if one region is
slightly denser than average, it pulls in matter from its surroundings and becomes
denser still. Gravity is **unstable**: small differences grow.

We describe a perturbation by its [[density-contrast|density contrast]]

$$\delta = \frac{\rho - \bar{\rho}}{\bar{\rho}}$$

where $\bar{\rho}$ is the average density. The CMB shows that at recombination
$\delta$ was only about $10^{-5}$ in ordinary matter. Today galaxies have
$\delta \sim 10^{6}$. This lesson explains how the first part of that journey
works.

## The Jeans criterion

Gravity is not the only force. Pressure resists compression. In 1902 **James Jeans**
compared the two for a static gas cloud.

- The time for gravity to collapse a region of density $\rho$ is
  $t_{\mathrm{ff}} \sim 1/\sqrt{G\rho}$ (the free-fall time).
- The time for pressure to respond across a size $\lambda$ is the sound-crossing
  time $\lambda / c_s$.

If sound crosses the region faster than gravity can collapse it, pressure wins and
the perturbation just oscillates. If not, gravity wins. The dividing line is the
[[jeans-length|Jeans length]]:

$$\lambda_J = c_s\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{G\rho}}$$

The mass inside a sphere of this size is the **Jeans mass** $M_J$. Regions heavier
than $M_J$ collapse; lighter ones oscillate like sound waves.

:::example The Jeans mass before and after recombination
**Before recombination**, baryons were locked to photons, and the sound speed was
about half the speed of light. The Jeans mass of the baryons was around
$10^{18}$–$10^{19}\ M_\odot$, larger than the richest galaxy clusters. Ordinary
matter could not collapse at all; it oscillated, producing the acoustic peaks of
lesson [L5.1](lesson:L5.1).

**After recombination**, neutral gas at 3 000 K had a sound speed of only about
6 km/s. The Jeans mass dropped by a factor of about $10^{13}$, to roughly
$10^{5}$–$10^{6}\ M_\odot$: the mass of a globular star cluster. From then on,
ordinary matter could fall into any structure larger than that.
:::

## Growth in an expanding universe

In a static cloud, an unstable perturbation grows **exponentially**, as
$\delta \propto e^{t/t_{\mathrm{ff}}}$. In the expanding universe the background
itself thins out while the perturbation tries to grow. For pressureless matter on
scales much larger than the Jeans length, linear theory gives

$$\ddot{\delta} + 2H\dot{\delta} = 4\pi G\bar{\rho}\,\delta$$

The right-hand side is gravity amplifying the perturbation. The term $2H\dot{\delta}$
acts like friction, called **Hubble drag**: expansion pulls matter apart and slows
the growth.

:::key Growth follows the scale factor
In a matter-dominated universe the growing solution is

$$\delta \propto a \propto t^{2/3}$$

Perturbations grow only as fast as the universe expands: a factor of 1 000 between
recombination and today, not exponentially.
:::

The [[growth-factor|linear growth factor]] $D(a)$ describes this for any
cosmology, with $\delta(a) = \delta_{\mathrm{initial}}\,D(a)/D_{\mathrm{initial}}$.

{{figure:growth_factor}}

- **Radiation era:** expansion is so fast that matter perturbations barely grow,
  only as $\ln a$. This stalling is called the **Mészáros effect**.
- **Matter era:** $D \propto a$.
- **Dark energy era:** expansion accelerates, Hubble drag wins and growth slowly
  freezes. In our universe perturbations today are about 22% smaller than they would
  be in a matter-only universe.

:::tip The growth rate
Cosmologists also measure the growth rate $f = d\ln D / d\ln a$. A very good
approximation is $f \approx \Omega_m(a)^{0.55}$. Galaxy surveys measure $f$ from the
way galaxies fall into overdense regions (redshift-space distortions), which tests
general relativity on cosmic scales.
:::

## Why dark matter is essential

Suppose the universe contained only ordinary matter.

- At recombination the baryon fluctuations were $\delta \approx 10^{-5}$.
- In our universe matter perturbations grew by a factor of roughly 900 from then
  until today.
- That gives $\delta \approx 10^{-2}$ today: still almost perfectly smooth. No
  galaxies, no stars, no us.

:::key Dark matter got a head start
Dark matter does not interact with photons, so it had no pressure and its
perturbations could grow from matter–radiation equality ($z \approx 3400$), long
before recombination. When the baryons were released, they fell into the dark matter
potential wells that were already much deeper. This is one of the strongest
arguments for dark matter (lesson [L3.2](lesson:L3.2)).
:::

## When linear theory breaks down

The linear equations assume $|\delta| \ll 1$. When $\delta$ approaches 1, a region
stops expanding, turns around and collapses into a gravitationally bound
[[dark-matter-halo|dark matter halo]]. Following this non-linear stage requires
the spherical collapse model and computer simulations, the subject of lesson
[L5.5](lesson:L5.5).

## Summary

- Density perturbations $\delta$ grow because gravity is unstable.
- Pressure resists collapse below the Jeans length $\lambda_J = c_s\sqrt{\pi/G\rho}$;
  the baryon Jeans mass fell from about $10^{18}\ M_\odot$ to $10^{5}\ M_\odot$ at
  recombination.
- In an expanding universe growth is slow: $\delta \propto a$ in the matter era,
  almost none in the radiation era, and freezing in the dark energy era.
- Baryons alone would grow only to $\delta \sim 10^{-2}$ today; dark matter's head start
  made galaxies possible.
