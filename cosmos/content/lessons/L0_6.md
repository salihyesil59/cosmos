## A question a child could ask

Why is the sky dark at night? The obvious answer is "because the Sun is on the
other side of the Earth". But think about the stars. If the universe were
infinite, unchanging and filled evenly with stars, the night sky should be
**blazing with light**. This contradiction is called
[[olbers-paradox|Olbers' paradox]], after the German astronomer **Heinrich
Olbers**, who discussed it in 1823. Kepler (1610), Halley and de Chéseaux (1744)
had already worried about it.

## The forest of stars

Imagine standing in a huge forest. Nearby trees are few but look wide; distant
trees look thin but there are many of them. If the forest is big enough, **every
line of sight ends on a tree trunk**, and you see a solid wall of wood in every
direction.

The same should happen with stars in an infinite universe: every line of sight
would eventually end on the surface of a star, and the whole sky would shine as
brightly as the surface of the Sun, day and night.

## The shell argument

Olbers' argument can also be made with numbers. Divide the space around us into
thin spherical shells of thickness $dr$, like the layers of an onion, and suppose
there are $n$ stars per unit volume, each with luminosity $L$.

- The shell at radius $r$ has volume $4\pi r^2\,dr$, so it holds
  $N = n\,4\pi r^2\,dr$ stars: the number **grows** as $r^2$.
- Each of those stars appears fainter by the inverse-square law
  (lesson [L1.1](lesson:L1.1)): its flux is $L/(4\pi r^2)$, which **falls** as
  $1/r^2$.

The light we receive from one shell is therefore

$$dF = n\,4\pi r^2\,dr \times \frac{L}{4\pi r^2} = n\,L\,dr$$

:::key Every shell contributes the same
The distance $r$ cancels out. A shell a billion light-years away sends us exactly
as much light as a shell of the same thickness nearby. Add up infinitely many
shells and the total brightness is infinite. In reality nearer stars would block
the farther ones, so the sky would "only" be as bright as a star's surface
everywhere.
:::

## A failed solution: dust

A tempting answer is that dust between the stars absorbs the distant light. Olbers
himself suggested this. It does not work: in an eternal universe the dust would
absorb energy until it became as hot as the starlight around it, and then it
would glow just as brightly. Absorption only delays the problem.

## The real solution: the universe has a finite age

The key assumptions were that the universe is **infinitely old** and
**unchanging**. Both are false.

1. **Light needs time to travel.** The universe is about 13.8 billion years old,
   so we can only receive light from a limited region: the
   [[observable-universe|observable universe]]. Light from farther away has not
   reached us yet.
2. **Stars do not live forever.** Stars shine for millions to billions of years
   and have existed only for the last ~13.5 billion years. There is simply not
   enough energy in all the stars to fill the universe with light as bright as a
   stellar surface.

How far would we need to see for lines of sight to hit a star? With about
$10^9$ Sun-like stars per cubic megaparsec (a realistic average), a straight line
travels about $10^{24}$ light-years before it meets a stellar surface. The
observable universe is some $10^{14}$ times smaller than that.

{{figure:olbers_sky_coverage}}

:::example A tiny fraction of the sky
Within the 13.8 billion light-years that light has had time to cross, the stars
cover only about $10^{-14}$ of the sky. The sky is dark because the universe is
too young and too sparsely filled with stars, not because the stars are hidden.
:::

:::history A poet's insight
The first person to state the correct solution was probably the writer **Edgar
Allan Poe**. In his 1848 essay *Eureka* he suggested that we see darkness in the
gaps between stars because light from beyond has not had time to reach us. **Lord
Kelvin** worked out the physics quantitatively in 1901.
:::

## What about the expanding universe?

Cosmic expansion also darkens the sky. Light from distant galaxies is redshifted
(lesson [L0.3](lesson:L0.3)), so each photon arrives with less energy, and photons
arrive less often. This makes distant sources dimmer than the simple
inverse-square law suggests. Detailed calculations show that expansion reduces the
night-sky brightness by roughly a factor of two or three, while the finite age of
the universe and the finite lifetimes of stars reduce it by an enormous factor.
Expansion helps, but it is not the main reason.

:::try S17 Choose "Infinitely old but expanding" in the Olbers' Paradox Simulator: redshift dimming alone can darken the sky only if the universe expands fast enough compared with the distance between stars.

## The sky is not completely dark

Look at the sky with microwave eyes and it **does glow uniformly** in every
direction. This is the cosmic microwave background, light released when the whole
universe was a hot glowing gas at about 3 000 K (lesson [L1.5](lesson:L1.5)).
Back then, every line of sight really did end on a glowing surface, just as
Olbers imagined. Expansion has since stretched that light by a factor of about
1 100, cooling it to 2.7 K, far too cold for our eyes to see.

:::note The extragalactic background light
All the galaxies that ever shone also create a faint diffuse glow, the
*extragalactic background light*. Measuring it tells astronomers how much light
all stars have produced over cosmic history.
:::

## Summary

- In an infinite, eternal, static universe full of stars, every line of sight
  would end on a star and the sky would be bright.
- Each shell of stars contributes equally, because $r^2$ more stars balance the
  $1/r^2$ dimming.
- Dust cannot solve the paradox: it would heat up and glow.
- The night sky is dark mainly because the universe has a finite age and stars
  have finite lifetimes; expansion helps a little.
- In microwaves the sky does glow: the cosmic microwave background.
