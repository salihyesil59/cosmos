## A prediction

If the universe was once hot and dense (lesson [L0.5](lesson:L0.5)), it must
have been filled with intense radiation, like the glow inside a furnace. As the
universe expanded, that radiation should have cooled but never disappeared.

In 1948 **Ralph Alpher** and **Robert Herman**, working with **George Gamow** on
how the Big Bang could make light elements, estimated that this leftover radiation
should today have a temperature of about **5 kelvin**, just above absolute zero.
Such cold radiation would glow at microwave wavelengths.

The prediction was largely forgotten. Radio technology of the time could not
easily detect it, and many astronomers were not convinced by the Big Bang model at
all.

:::history A clue nobody understood
In 1941 Andrew McKellar found that cyanogen (CN) molecules in interstellar space
were slightly excited, as if bathed in radiation at about 2.3 K. Nobody connected
this with cosmology until after 1965.
:::

## An annoying noise

In 1964 **Arno Penzias** and **Robert Wilson** at Bell Telephone Laboratories in
Holmdel, New Jersey, wanted to use a large horn-shaped antenna, originally built
for early satellite communication, for radio astronomy. To do so, they had to
understand every source of noise in their receiver.

They found an unexplained excess signal at a wavelength of 7.35 cm, equivalent to
radiation at about **3.5 K**. It was:

- the same in **every direction** in the sky,
- the same **day and night**,
- the same in **every season** over the whole year.

They checked everything. They even removed a pair of pigeons nesting in the horn
and cleaned out what they politely called "white dielectric material". The noise
remained.

## Two teams meet

Only about 50 kilometres away, at Princeton University, **Robert Dicke**, **Jim
Peebles**, **Peter Roll** and **David Wilkinson** had independently revived the
idea of a relic radiation and were building a detector to search for it. When
Penzias heard of their work, he called Dicke. After the call Dicke reportedly told
his colleagues: "Boys, we've been scooped."

In 1965 the two groups published companion papers: Penzias and Wilson reported the
measurement, and the Princeton group explained it as the
[[cmb|cosmic microwave background]] (CMB). Penzias and Wilson received the 1978
Nobel Prize in Physics.

:::key The oldest light in the universe
The CMB was released about 380 000 years after the Big Bang, when the universe
became transparent. It has travelled for 13.8 billion years, and its wavelength
has been stretched about 1 100 times by cosmic expansion, turning a 3 000 K
orange glow into 2.7 K microwaves. The details are in lesson [L4.4](lesson:L4.4).
:::

## The end of the steady state theory

Why was this discovery so important? The
[[steady-state-theory|steady state theory]] said the universe has always looked
the same. It had no natural way to produce a smooth sea of radiation with the same
temperature everywhere. The hot Big Bang predicted exactly that.

:::note A blackbody fingerprint
A hot, dense early universe must produce a [[blackbody|blackbody]] spectrum, the
characteristic shape of light from matter in thermal equilibrium. Measuring the
spectrum at many wavelengths would therefore be a decisive test. Ground-based and
rocket experiments in the following decades gradually confirmed the shape, but
the atmosphere absorbs much of the microwave range, so a satellite was needed.
:::

## COBE: a perfect blackbody and the first ripples

NASA's **Cosmic Background Explorer (COBE)** launched in 1989.

- Its FIRAS instrument, led by **John Mather**, showed in 1990 that the CMB is a
  nearly perfect blackbody with a temperature now measured as $T_0 = 2.7255$ K.
  When the spectrum was shown at a meeting of astronomers, the audience gave it a
  standing ovation.
- Its DMR instrument, led by **George Smoot**, found in 1992 tiny temperature
  differences of about **one part in 100 000** between different directions.
  These ripples are the seeds from which galaxies and the cosmic web grew (lesson
  [L1.4](lesson:L1.4)).

Mather and Smoot shared the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physics.

## Mapping the ripples

After COBE, experiments mapped the ripples in ever finer detail:

- **BOOMERanG** and **MAXIMA** (balloon experiments, 2000) measured the typical
  size of the hot and cold spots, about 1°, showing that space is flat (lesson
  [L2.5](lesson:L2.5)).
- **WMAP** (NASA, 2001–2010) produced full-sky maps and pinned down the age and
  contents of the universe.
- **Planck** (ESA, 2009–2013) made the most detailed maps so far, measuring the
  basic cosmological parameters to about 1% (lesson [L3.4](lesson:L3.4)).

:::note The CMB is all around you
There are about 411 CMB photons in every cubic centimetre of space, including the
room you are in. In the days of analogue television, a small part of the "snow"
on a TV tuned between channels was caused by the CMB.
:::

## Summary

- The hot Big Bang predicted a leftover radiation of a few kelvin (Alpher and
  Herman, 1948).
- Penzias and Wilson discovered it by accident in 1965 as an unexplained,
  direction-independent noise; the Princeton group explained it.
- The CMB decided the debate in favour of the Big Bang over the steady state theory.
- COBE showed a perfect 2.7255 K blackbody and tiny ripples; WMAP and Planck turned
  those ripples into precise measurements of the universe.
