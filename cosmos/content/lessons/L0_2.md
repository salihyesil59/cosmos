## Why new units?

Kilometres are fine on Earth, but the distance to the nearest star is about
40 000 000 000 000 km. Numbers like this are hard to read and easy to get wrong.
Astronomers therefore use larger units, each suited to a particular scale.

## The astronomical unit (AU)

The [[astronomical-unit|astronomical unit]] is roughly the average distance
between the Earth and the Sun. Since 2012 it has been defined exactly:

$$1\ \mathrm{AU} = 149\,597\,870\,700\ \mathrm{m} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}$$

It is ideal for the Solar System: Jupiter orbits at about 5.2 AU, Neptune at
about 30 AU.

## The light-year (ly)

A [[light-year|light-year]] is the distance light travels in one year:

$$1\ \mathrm{ly} = c \times 1\ \mathrm{year} \approx 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \mathrm{m} \approx 63\,000\ \mathrm{AU}$$

Here $c \approx 300\,000$ km/s is the speed of light.

:::warning A light-year is not a time
Despite the word "year", a light-year measures **distance**. Saying "the star is
4 light-years away" means light needs 4 years to reach us from it.
:::

## The parsec (pc)

Professional astronomers mostly use the [[parsec|parsec]]. It comes from the way
the nearest star distances are measured: [[parallax|parallax]].

As the Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift slightly against very
distant background stars, just as your thumb seems to jump when you look at it
with one eye and then the other. Half of that yearly shift is the **parallax
angle** $p$.

A star has a distance of **1 parsec** if its parallax angle is exactly one
arcsecond (1/3600 of a degree). Because the angle is tiny, the distance is
simply inversely proportional to it:

$$d\,[\mathrm{pc}] = \frac{1}{p\,[\mathrm{arcsec}]}$$

$$1\ \mathrm{pc} \approx 3.26\ \mathrm{ly} \approx 3.09 \times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{m}$$

:::example Worked example
The nearest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri, has a parallax of 0.768
arcseconds. Its distance is $d = 1/0.768 \approx 1.30$ pc, or about
$1.30 \times 3.26 \approx 4.24$ light-years.
:::

## Kiloparsecs, megaparsecs and gigaparsecs

For larger distances we add the usual metric prefixes:

| Unit | Value | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 kpc (kiloparsec) | 1 000 pc ≈ 3 260 ly | Sizes of galaxies; the Sun is 8 kpc from the Galactic centre |
| 1 Mpc ([[megaparsec]]) | 1 000 000 pc ≈ 3.26 million ly | Distances between galaxies; Andromeda is 0.78 Mpc away |
| 1 Gpc (gigaparsec) | 1 000 Mpc ≈ 3.26 billion ly | Cosmological distances |

The megaparsec is *the* unit of cosmology. You will meet it constantly, for
example in the Hubble constant, measured in kilometres per second per
megaparsec.

## Converting between units

A handy summary:

$$1\ \mathrm{pc} = 3.26\ \mathrm{ly} = 206\,265\ \mathrm{AU} = 3.09 \times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{m}$$

:::tip Estimating in your head
One megaparsec is about 3 million light-years, or $3 \times 10^{22}$ metres.
These rounded values are usually good enough to check whether an answer makes
sense.
:::

## Summary

- **AU** ≈ 150 million km: distances inside the Solar System.
- **Light-year** ≈ 9.46 trillion km: the distance light travels in a year.
- **Parsec** ≈ 3.26 ly: defined through a parallax of one arcsecond.
- **Megaparsec** ≈ 3.26 million ly: the everyday unit of cosmology.
