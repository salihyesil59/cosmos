## A cosmic nuclear reactor

About one second after the Big Bang the universe was at $10^{10}$ K and contained
photons, neutrinos, electrons, positrons and a small sprinkling of protons and
neutrons. Within the next twenty minutes some of these protons and neutrons fused
into light nuclei. This process is called Big Bang
[[nucleosynthesis|nucleosynthesis]] (BBN).

:::key A prediction confirmed
BBN predicts the amounts of helium-4, deuterium, helium-3 and lithium-7 using
only well-tested nuclear physics and one unknown number: the density of ordinary
matter. The agreement between prediction and observation is a cornerstone of the
Big Bang theory.
:::

## Step 1: freezing the neutron-to-proton ratio

At very high temperatures, weak interactions with neutrinos and electrons
constantly turn protons into neutrons and back. A neutron is slightly heavier than
a proton, by $\Delta m\,c^2 = 1.29$ MeV, so in equilibrium there are fewer
neutrons:

$$\frac{n}{p} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta m\,c^2}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right)$$

These weak reactions freeze out (lesson [L4.2](lesson:L4.2)) at about
$k_{\mathrm{B}}T \approx 0.8$ MeV, when $n/p \approx e^{-1.29/0.8} \approx 1/5$.
Afterwards free neutrons slowly decay, with a mean lifetime of about 880 seconds,
into protons.

## Step 2: the deuterium bottleneck

The first step towards heavier nuclei is deuterium (one proton plus one neutron),
bound by only 2.2 MeV. At first, high-energy photons break deuterium apart as soon
as it forms.

:::note Why wait so long?
The typical photon energy drops below 2.2 MeV after less than a minute, but there
are about $1.6 \times 10^9$ photons per [[baryon|baryon]]. Even when the average
photon is too weak, the rare energetic photons in the tail of the blackbody
spectrum are numerous enough to destroy deuterium. Deuterium survives only when
$k_{\mathrm{B}}T \approx 0.07$ MeV ($8 \times 10^{8}$ K), about three minutes after
the Big Bang.
:::

By then neutron decay has lowered the ratio to $n/p \approx 1/7$.

## Step 3: helium

Once deuterium survives, reactions quickly build [[helium-4|helium-4]], the most
tightly bound light nucleus. Practically all remaining neutrons end up in
helium-4, which contains 2 neutrons and 2 protons. Counting masses, the helium mass
fraction $Y_p$ is

$$Y_p = \frac{2\,(n/p)}{1 + n/p} = \frac{2 \times 1/7}{1 + 1/7} = 0.25$$

:::example Check the arithmetic
Take 16 nucleons: with $n/p = 1/7$ there are 2 neutrons and 14 protons. The 2
neutrons combine with 2 protons into one helium nucleus of mass 4. So 4 of the 16
units of mass, 25%, are helium; the other 12 protons remain hydrogen nuclei.
:::

Observations of the oldest, least polluted gas clouds give $Y_p \approx 0.245$, in
excellent agreement.

## Why no heavier elements?

There are no stable nuclei with 5 or 8 nucleons, which blocks the easy routes to
heavier elements. Moreover, the universe keeps cooling and thinning, so after
about 20 minutes the nuclei no longer have enough energy to fuse. Only tiny traces
of lithium-7 were made. All carbon, oxygen, iron and the other heavy elements were
forged much later in stars.

## Deuterium: a baryon density meter

{{figure:bbn_abundances}}

Leftover [[deuterium|deuterium]] is very sensitive to the density of ordinary
matter: the more baryons, the more efficiently deuterium is burned into helium, so
**more baryons means less deuterium**.

Measurements of deuterium in distant gas clouds, seen in absorption against
quasars, give $\mathrm{D/H} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-5}$. BBN then requires

$$\Omega_{\mathrm{b}} h^2 \approx 0.022 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Omega_{\mathrm{b}} \approx 0.05$$

:::key Two methods, one answer
The cosmic microwave background, formed 380 000 years later by completely
different physics, gives the same value of $\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^2$. This is why we
are confident that ordinary matter is only about 5% of the universe, and that
dark matter must be something else.
:::

The figure is often called the Schramm plot, after the cosmologist David Schramm.
All four elements are predicted by a single number, the baryon-to-photon ratio
$\eta$, and the green band shows the value measured independently from the CMB.

:::try S11 In the BBN Abundance Explorer, press "From deuterium" and check whether the result lands inside the CMB band. Then find the lithium dip.

:::warning The lithium problem
Observed lithium-7 in old stars is about three times lower than BBN predicts. It
is not yet clear whether this points to stellar physics that destroys lithium,
measurement issues, or new physics.
:::

## Summary

- The neutron-to-proton ratio froze at about 1/5 and fell to about 1/7 by neutron
  decay.
- The deuterium bottleneck delayed nucleosynthesis until about 3 minutes.
- Nearly all neutrons ended up in helium-4: $Y_p \approx 25\%$ by mass.
- Deuterium abundance measures the baryon density, $\Omega_b \approx 5\%$, in
  agreement with the CMB.
