## A stretching grid

Imagine drawing a grid on the universe, with galaxies sitting at the grid points.
As the universe expands, the grid stretches but galaxies keep their grid
positions (ignoring their small peculiar velocities). Their **coordinates** stay
the same; only the spacing of the grid grows.

The cosmological principle says this stretching must be the same everywhere and
in every direction. So one number describes it at each moment: the
[[scale-factor|scale factor]] $a(t)$.

## Comoving and proper distance

- The **[[comoving-distance|comoving distance]]** $\chi$ is the distance measured
  on the grid. It does not change as the universe expands.
- The **[[proper-distance|proper distance]]** $D$ is the actual physical distance
  you would measure with a (very long) ruler at a given moment.

They are related by the scale factor:

$$D(t) = a(t)\,\chi$$

By convention $a = 1$ **today** ($t_0$), so comoving distances equal today's
proper distances. When the universe was half its current size, $a = 0.5$, and all
proper distances between galaxies were half of today's.

:::example Worked example
Two galaxies are 100 Mpc apart today. When $a$ was 0.25, they were
$0.25 \times 100 = 25$ Mpc apart. When $a$ reaches 2 in the future, they will be
200 Mpc apart. Their comoving separation remains 100 Mpc throughout.
:::

## From the scale factor to Hubble's law

How fast does the proper distance grow? Differentiate $D = a\chi$ with respect to
time (the dot means $d/dt$):

$$v = \dot{D} = \dot{a}\,\chi = \frac{\dot{a}}{a}\,(a\chi) = H(t)\,D$$

with the **Hubble parameter**

$$H(t) = \frac{\dot{a}}{a}$$

This is the Hubble–Lemaître law, derived from uniform stretching. The Hubble
constant is simply today's value, $H_0 = H(t_0)$. The Hubble "constant" is the
same everywhere in space at a given time, but it **changes with time**.

:::key One function describes the universe
Everything about the expansion is contained in $a(t)$. The rest of this level
answers the question: what determines how $a(t)$ changes?
:::

## Faster than light?

Because $v = H_0 D$ grows without limit, galaxies beyond the **Hubble distance**

$$D_H = \frac{c}{H_0} \approx 4\,300\ \mathrm{Mpc} \approx 14\ \mathrm{billion\ light\ years}$$

recede faster than light. Does this break relativity?

:::warning No speed limit is broken
Special relativity forbids objects from moving *through space* faster than light
relative to each other at the same place. Recession velocity is not motion
through space; it is the growth of space between distant objects. We routinely
observe galaxies whose light was emitted when they were receding faster than
light.
:::

## The scale factor and redshift (a preview)

As light travels through expanding space, its wavelength is stretched along with
everything else. Light emitted when the scale factor was $a$ arrives today with

$$1 + z = \frac{1}{a}$$

A galaxy at $z = 1$ emitted its light when the universe was half its present
size. This is the subject of the next lesson.

:::try S7 Watch galaxies keep their grid positions while the distances between them grow.

## Summary

- The scale factor $a(t)$ describes the stretching of the universe; $a = 1$ today.
- Proper distance $D = a\chi$; comoving distance $\chi$ stays fixed.
- The Hubble parameter is $H = \dot{a}/a$, and $v = HD$ follows automatically.
- Beyond $c/H_0$ galaxies recede faster than light without violating relativity.
