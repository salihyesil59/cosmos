## The question

The scale factor $a(t)$ contains the whole history of the universe. What decides
how it evolves? In 1922 the Russian mathematician **Alexander Friedmann** found
the answer by solving Einstein's equations for a homogeneous, isotropic universe.
Lemaître found the same equations independently in 1927.

The full derivation uses general relativity, but a beautifully simple Newtonian
argument gives the same result. Let us follow it.

## An expanding sphere

Take a uniform universe with mass density $\rho$. Pick any point as the centre
(every point is equivalent) and draw a sphere of comoving radius $\chi$. Its
proper radius is $R = a\chi$, and the mass inside is

$$M = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\rho\,R^3$$

Now follow a small galaxy of mass $m$ on the surface. A remarkable theorem of
Newton (the *shell theorem*) says that the matter outside the sphere exerts no net
force on it, so only $M$ matters. As the sphere expands, no matter crosses its
surface, so $M$ stays constant.

## Energy conservation

The galaxy on the surface of the sphere keeps a constant total energy: the faster
it moves outwards, the more it has to spend climbing out of the gravity of the
mass inside. Writing that statement down and tidying it up gives the equation that
governs the whole universe.

:::math The algebra, step by step
The galaxy's kinetic plus gravitational potential energy is constant:

$$\frac{1}{2} m \dot{R}^2 - \frac{G M m}{R} = E$$

Substitute $R = a\chi$ and $M = \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho a^3 \chi^3$, then divide by
$\frac{1}{2} m a^2 \chi^2$:

$$\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho + \frac{2E}{m\chi^2 a^2}$$

The last term is a constant divided by $a^2$. Writing that constant as $-kc^2$
gives the **Friedmann equation**:

$$H^2 = \left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho - \frac{k c^2}{a^2}$$
:::

:::key The first Friedmann equation
The expansion rate squared is set by the density of the universe and by a
constant $k$. In general relativity, $\rho$ includes all forms of energy
($\rho = \varepsilon/c^2$), and $k$ describes the **curvature of space**
(lesson [L2.5](lesson:L2.5)).
:::

## Three possible fates (for matter only)

The energy $E$, and therefore $k$, decides the future, just like a ball thrown
upward from the Earth:

| Energy | Curvature | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| $E > 0$ | $k < 0$ (open) | Expands forever, like a ball faster than escape velocity |
| $E = 0$ | $k = 0$ (flat) | Expands forever, just barely, slowing towards zero speed |
| $E < 0$ | $k > 0$ (closed) | Expansion stops and reverses: a **[[big-crunch|Big Crunch]]** |

This neat correspondence between geometry and fate holds when the universe
contains only matter. Dark energy breaks it, as we will see in Level 3.

## The acceleration equation

Differentiating, or applying Newton's second law to the galaxy directly, gives
how the expansion speeds up or slows down. General relativity adds one important
ingredient: **pressure** $p$ also gravitates.

$$\frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\right)$$

For ordinary matter and radiation, $\rho$ and $p$ are positive, so $\ddot{a} < 0$:
gravity decelerates the expansion. A substance with strongly **negative
pressure**, $p < -\rho c^2/3$, makes $\ddot{a} > 0$: accelerated expansion. Keep
this in mind for dark energy.

## The fluid equation

As the universe expands, the density changes. The first law of thermodynamics
applied to an expanding volume gives

$$\dot{\rho} + 3\,\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\left(\rho + \frac{p}{c^2}\right) = 0$$

For pressureless matter ($p = 0$) this means $\rho \propto a^{-3}$: the same mass
spread over a volume that grows as $a^3$. Together, the Friedmann, acceleration
and fluid equations determine $a(t)$ once we know what the universe contains.

## Example: the Einstein–de Sitter universe

Consider a flat universe ($k = 0$) containing only matter, $\rho = \rho_0 a^{-3}$.
The Friedmann equation becomes $\dot{a}^2 \propto a^{-1}$, which is solved by

$$a(t) = \left(\frac{t}{t_0}\right)^{2/3}$$

Then $H = \dot{a}/a = 2/(3t)$, so the age of this universe is

$$t_0 = \frac{2}{3}\,\frac{1}{H_0} \approx 9.3\ \mathrm{Gyr} \quad (H_0 = 70\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}})$$

:::warning The age problem
9.3 billion years is younger than the oldest stars, which are about 12–13 billion
years old. This "age problem" was one of the hints, before 1998, that the
matter-only model could not be the whole story.
:::

## Summary

- Newtonian energy conservation for an expanding sphere gives the Friedmann
  equation $H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - kc^2/a^2$.
- The constant $k$ represents spatial curvature and, for matter only, decides the
  fate of the universe.
- The acceleration equation shows that pressure gravitates; negative pressure
  can cause acceleration.
- A flat matter-only universe expands as $a \propto t^{2/3}$ and has age
  $\frac{2}{3}H_0^{-1}$.
