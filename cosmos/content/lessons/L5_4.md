## Clumpiness, scale by scale

The universe is lumpy on some scales and smooth on others. To describe this,
cosmologists break the density field $\delta(\mathbf{x})$ into waves of different
wavelengths, exactly like splitting sound into frequencies. Each wave has a
**wavenumber** $k = 2\pi/\lambda$: small $k$ means large scales, large $k$ small
scales.

The [[matter-power-spectrum|matter power spectrum]] $P(k)$ is the average squared
amplitude of the waves with wavenumber $k$:

$$\langle \delta_{\mathbf{k}}\,\delta^*_{\mathbf{k}'} \rangle = (2\pi)^3\,\delta_D(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}')\,P(k)$$

In words: a large $P(k)$ means strong density fluctuations on scales of about
$2\pi/k$. The quantity $k^3P(k)/2\pi^2$ is the variance of $\delta$ per logarithmic
interval of scale.

:::note Units
Wavenumbers are usually quoted in $h$/Mpc and $P(k)$ in (Mpc/$h$)³, where
$h = H_0/100$ km/s/Mpc. This removes the uncertainty in the Hubble constant from the
comparison of surveys.
:::

For a **Gaussian random field**, which the early universe was to excellent
precision, the power spectrum contains *all* statistical information. It is the
Fourier-space twin of the correlation function of lesson [L5.2](lesson:L5.2).

## The primordial spectrum

Inflation predicts that the initial fluctuations had a power-law spectrum

$$P_{\mathrm{prim}}(k) \propto k^{n_s}$$

with $n_s$ close to 1. The case $n_s = 1$ is called **scale-invariant**
(Harrison–Zel'dovich): the gravitational potential fluctuations have the same
strength on every scale. Planck measures $n_s = 0.965 \pm 0.004$, slightly *below* 1,
a key clue about inflation.

## Processing: the transfer function

The spectrum we see today is not the primordial one. Each mode's growth depended on
**when it entered the horizon**, which the [[transfer-function|transfer function]]
$T(k)$ describes:

$$P(k, z) = A\,k^{n_s}\,T^2(k)\,D^2(z)$$

- **Large scales** ($k < k_{\mathrm{eq}}$) entered the horizon after
  matter–radiation equality. They grew steadily the whole time: $T \approx 1$ and
  $P \propto k^{n_s}$.
- **Small scales** ($k > k_{\mathrm{eq}}$) entered during the radiation era, when
  growth stalled (the Mészáros effect of lesson [L5.3](lesson:L5.3)). The smaller
  the scale, the longer it waited: $P$ falls roughly as $k^{-3}$.

{{figure:matter_power_spectrum}}

:::key The turnover measures matter density
The peak of $P(k)$ sits at the scale that entered the horizon at matter–radiation
equality, $k_{\mathrm{eq}} \approx 0.016\ h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, a wavelength of about
400 Mpc/$h$. Its position depends on $\Omega_m h$, so the shape of the power
spectrum measures the total matter density. In 1990 the APM galaxy survey found more
power on large scales than a universe with $\Omega_m = 1$ allows: one of the first
hints of a low-density universe.
:::

Two smaller features are imprinted on top:

- **Baryon acoustic oscillations**: wiggles of a few percent near
  $k \approx 0.05$–$0.3\ h$/Mpc (lesson [L5.2](lesson:L5.2)).
- **Baryon suppression**: before recombination baryons could not cluster, so a
  universe with more baryons has less small-scale power.

## The amplitude: σ8

Instead of $A$, cosmologists quote [[sigma8|σ8]]: the root-mean-square fluctuation of
matter in spheres of radius 8 Mpc/$h$, calculated with linear theory. That scale
was chosen because the galaxy number fluctuations on it are close to 1.

$$\sigma_8^2 = \int \frac{k^3 P(k)}{2\pi^2}\,W^2(k R)\,\frac{dk}{k}, \qquad R = 8\ h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}$$

where $W$ is the Fourier transform of a sphere. Planck gives $\sigma_8 \approx 0.81$.

:::warning The S8 tension
Weak gravitational lensing surveys (lesson [L5.6](lesson:L5.6)) find
$S_8 = \sigma_8\sqrt{\Omega_m/0.3}$ a few percent lower than predicted from the CMB.
Whether this is a statistical fluke, a systematic error, or new physics (for
example, massive neutrinos or interacting dark matter) is an active question.
:::

## Growth over time

All linear modes grow together, so the whole spectrum simply moves up as
$D^2(z)$: at redshift 2 the power was about 5.7 times lower than today, at redshift 6
about 30 times lower. On small scales today, $\delta$ has exceeded 1 and linear theory
fails. There, gravity transfers power from large to small scales and $P(k)$ rises
above the linear prediction.

:::try S13 Change the spectral index n in the N-body simulator. More negative values put more power on large scales and give big coherent filaments; values near zero give many small clumps.

## How P(k) is measured

- **Galaxy redshift surveys** (SDSS, 2dFGRS, DESI, Euclid) measure the clustering of
  galaxies. Galaxies are **biased** tracers: $P_{\mathrm{gal}} = b^2 P$, with a bias
  $b$ of order 1–3 that must be modelled.
- **Weak lensing** measures the matter itself, dark matter included.
- **The Lyman-α forest**: absorption by hydrogen clouds in quasar spectra probes
  small scales at redshifts 2–5.
- **The CMB** measures the primordial spectrum on the largest scales.

Together they trace the same ΛCDM spectrum over more than three decades in scale,
from thousands of Mpc down to about 1 Mpc.

## Summary

- $P(k)$ measures how strongly matter fluctuates on the scale $2\pi/k$.
- The primordial spectrum is almost scale-invariant, $P \propto k^{n_s}$ with
  $n_s = 0.965$.
- Modes that entered the horizon in the radiation era grew less: the spectrum peaks
  at $k_{\mathrm{eq}} \approx 0.016\ h$/Mpc and falls on small scales.
- BAO wiggles and baryon suppression are imprinted on top.
- The amplitude is quoted as $\sigma_8 \approx 0.81$; weak lensing finds slightly lower
  values (the S8 tension).
