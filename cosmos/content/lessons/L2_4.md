## A natural yardstick for density

Set $k = 0$ in the Friedmann equation, $H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho$, and solve for
the density. For a given expansion rate $H$, a flat universe needs exactly the
[[critical-density|critical density]]:

$$\rho_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{3H^2}{8\pi G}$$

With $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc,

$$\rho_{\mathrm{c},0} \approx 9.2 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$$

:::example How dense is that?
A hydrogen atom has a mass of $1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, so the critical density
corresponds to about **5.5 hydrogen atoms per cubic metre**. The best vacuum made
in laboratories still contains millions of molecules per cubic metre. On average,
the universe is incredibly empty.
:::

## Density parameters

It is convenient to measure every density in units of the critical density. The
[[density-parameter|density parameter]] is

$$\Omega = \frac{\rho}{\rho_{\mathrm{c}}}$$

Dividing the Friedmann equation by $H^2$ gives a remarkably simple result:

$$1 = \Omega - \frac{kc^2}{a^2 H^2} \equiv \Omega + \Omega_k$$

where $\Omega_k = -kc^2/(a^2H^2)$ is the **curvature parameter**. So:

| Total density | Curvature | Geometry |
|---|---|---|
| $\Omega > 1$ | $k > 0$, $\Omega_k < 0$ | closed |
| $\Omega = 1$ | $k = 0$, $\Omega_k = 0$ | flat |
| $\Omega < 1$ | $k < 0$, $\Omega_k > 0$ | open |

:::key Weighing the universe decides its geometry
By measuring the total density of the universe and comparing it with the critical
density, we learn the geometry of space. Observations find $\Omega$ equal to 1
within about 0.2%: the universe is flat, or extremely close to it.
:::

## Several components

The universe contains different ingredients, each with its own density
parameter today (subscript 0):

- $\Omega_{\mathrm{r},0}$ for radiation (photons and neutrinos),
- $\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}$ for matter (ordinary and dark),
- $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$ for dark energy.

Each dilutes differently as the universe expands: matter as $a^{-3}$, radiation
as $a^{-4}$ and a cosmological constant not at all (you will see why in lesson
[L3.1](lesson:L3.1)). Curvature behaves like $a^{-2}$. Putting this into the
Friedmann equation gives its most useful form:

$$H^2(a) = H_0^2\left[\Omega_{\mathrm{r},0}\,a^{-4} + \Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}\,a^{-3} + \Omega_{k,0}\,a^{-2} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\right]$$

With $a = 1/(1+z)$ we can compute the expansion rate at any redshift. The bracket
is often written $E^2(z)$, so that $H(z) = H_0\,E(z)$.

:::note Today's values
The Planck satellite (2018) finds approximately $\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0} = 0.31$,
$\Omega_{\Lambda,0} = 0.69$, $\Omega_{\mathrm{r},0} = 9 \times 10^{-5}$ and
$|\Omega_{k,0}| < 0.002$.
:::

## The flatness puzzle

The density parameters themselves change with time. For a matter-dominated
universe,

$$\Omega(a) - 1 \propto a$$

So any small deviation from flatness **grows** as the universe expands. For
$\Omega$ to be within 0.2% of 1 today, it must have been equal to 1 to about one
part in $10^{60}$ in the very early universe. Why was the universe tuned so
precisely? This *flatness problem* is one of the motivations for the theory of
cosmic inflation, an advanced topic in this course.

:::try S2 Set Ωm and ΩΛ so their sum differs from 1, and watch the geometry label change.

## Summary

- The critical density $\rho_c = 3H^2/8\pi G$ is about 5 hydrogen atoms per cubic
  metre today.
- Density parameters $\Omega = \rho/\rho_c$ satisfy $\Omega + \Omega_k = 1$.
- $\Omega > 1$: closed; $\Omega = 1$: flat; $\Omega < 1$: open.
- $H^2 = H_0^2[\Omega_r a^{-4} + \Omega_m a^{-3} + \Omega_k a^{-2} + \Omega_\Lambda]$
  describes the expansion at any time.
