## Stretching light

Think of a light wave as crests painted on the expanding grid. While the light
travels from a distant galaxy to us, the grid keeps stretching, and the distance
between neighbouring crests (the wavelength) stretches by exactly the same factor
as all other distances.

If light was emitted with wavelength $\lambda_{\mathrm{emit}}$ when the scale
factor was $a_{\mathrm{emit}}$, it arrives today ($a = 1$) with

$$\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{emit}}} = \frac{a_{\mathrm{now}}}{a_{\mathrm{emit}}} = \frac{1}{a_{\mathrm{emit}}}$$

Using the definition of redshift from lesson [L0.3](lesson:L0.3),
$1 + z = \lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{emit}}$, we obtain the key relation of
[[cosmological-redshift|cosmological redshift]]:

$$1 + z = \frac{1}{a_{\mathrm{emit}}}$$

:::key Redshift measures size
A redshift tells us directly **how much the universe has expanded** since the
light was emitted. At $z = 1$ the universe was half its present size; at $z = 3$
a quarter; at $z = 1100$ (the cosmic microwave background) about a thousandth.
:::

| Redshift z | Scale factor a | Universe size relative to today |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | today |
| 0.5 | 0.67 | two thirds |
| 1 | 0.5 | half |
| 3 | 0.25 | a quarter |
| 10 | 0.091 | about one eleventh |
| 1100 | 0.00091 | about one thousandth |

## Not a Doppler shift

A Doppler shift comes from the relative motion of source and observer **at the
moments of emission and reception**. Cosmological redshift is different: it
accumulates *during the journey*, as space expands.

Some consequences:

- Cosmological redshift depends only on the ratio of scale factors, not on how
  the expansion happened in between.
- There is no upper limit: $z$ grows without bound as $a_{\mathrm{emit}} \to 0$.
  Galaxies have been observed beyond $z = 14$.
- Applying the special-relativistic Doppler formula to a high-redshift galaxy
  gives a "velocity" below $c$, but that number has no clear physical meaning.

:::warning Common mistake
It is wrong to conclude that a galaxy at $z = 3$ moves at 3 times the speed of
light, or at 88% of it as the relativistic Doppler formula would suggest. Its
recession velocity depends on its distance and the expansion history, which we
calculate with the Friedmann equations.
:::

For small redshifts ($z \ll 1$) the two pictures agree: $z \approx v/c$ with
$v = H_0 D$. That is why Hubble could use the Doppler formula for nearby galaxies.

## Time dilation and energy

Expansion stretches everything about the light, not just its wavelength:

- **Time intervals** are stretched too. A supernova at $z = 1$ appears to fade
  twice as slowly as an identical nearby one; this has been observed.
- The **energy of each photon** $E = hc/\lambda$ decreases by the factor $1+z$.

:::note Where does the energy go?
In an expanding universe, energy is not globally conserved in the simple way it
is in a laboratory, because the background spacetime changes with time. This is
a subtle but well-understood feature of general relativity.
:::

## Redshift as a clock

Because redshift maps onto the scale factor, and the scale factor changes with
time, astronomers use redshift as a clock: "at redshift 6" means "when the universe
was 1/7 of its current size", which for our universe was about 900 million years
after the Big Bang.

## Summary

- Expansion stretches wavelengths: $1 + z = 1/a_{\mathrm{emit}}$.
- Redshift directly measures how much the universe has grown since emission.
- Cosmological redshift is not a Doppler shift; it builds up during the journey.
- Time intervals stretch and photon energies drop by the factor $1 + z$.
