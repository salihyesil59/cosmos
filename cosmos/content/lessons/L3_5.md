## From expansion rate to age

The Hubble parameter tells us how fast the universe grows: $H = \dot{a}/a$.
Turning this around, the time needed for the scale factor to grow by a small
amount $da$ is

$$dt = \frac{da}{\dot{a}} = \frac{da}{a\,H(a)}$$

Adding up these small steps from the Big Bang ($a = 0$) to today ($a = 1$) gives
the [[age-of-universe|age of the universe]]:

$$t_0 = \int_0^1 \frac{da}{a\,H(a)} = \frac{1}{H_0}\int_0^1 \frac{da}{a\,E(a)}$$

where, from lesson [L2.4](lesson:L2.4),

$$E(a) = \sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm{r},0}\,a^{-4} + \Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}\,a^{-3} + \Omega_{k,0}\,a^{-2} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}$$

:::key Two ingredients
The age is the [[hubble-time|Hubble time]] $1/H_0$ multiplied by a pure number
that depends only on the **contents** of the universe (the Ω values). Measuring
$H_0$ sets the overall scale; measuring the Ω values sets the factor.
:::

The same formula with the upper limit $a = 1/(1+z)$ gives the age of the universe
when light was emitted at redshift $z$.

## Exact answers for simple universes

For a few universes the integral can be solved with pen and paper.

**Empty universe** ($\Omega = 0$, the Milne model). Then $E = a^{-1}$ and the
integrand is 1:

$$t_0 = \frac{1}{H_0}$$

The expansion never slows down, so the Hubble time is exactly the age.

**Flat, matter only** (Einstein–de Sitter). Then $E = a^{-3/2}$:

$$t_0 = \frac{1}{H_0}\int_0^1 a^{1/2}\,da = \frac{2}{3}\,\frac{1}{H_0}$$

Gravity slowed the expansion, so the universe needed less time to reach today's
size. With $H_0 = 67.7$ km/s/Mpc this is only **9.6 billion years**.

**Flat, radiation only.** $E = a^{-2}$ gives $t_0 = \frac{1}{2H_0}$. Radiation
decelerates the expansion even more strongly.

**Flat ΛCDM, ignoring radiation.** The integral can still be done exactly:

$$t_0 = \frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}}\,\sinh^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_\Lambda}{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}}\right)$$

For $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} = 0.31$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.69$ this gives
$t_0 = 0.955/H_0 \approx$ **13.8 billion years**. Radiation changes the result by
less than 0.1%, because it only mattered during the first few hundred thousand
years.

## Seeing the integral

{{figure:age_integrand}}

The curve shows $1/(aH)$: the time the universe spends while the scale factor grows
by one unit. The area under the curve is the age.

- Near $a = 0$ the expansion was extremely fast, so very little time passed.
- In the matter-only universe (dashed) the curve always rises: the expansion keeps
  slowing down.
- With dark energy the curve reaches a maximum around $a \approx 0.6$ and then falls
  again as the expansion accelerates. The extra area compared with the dashed curve
  is why ΛCDM is older.

:::example A numerical estimate by hand
Split the range $0 \leq a \leq 1$ into five equal steps and evaluate the integrand at
the middle of each step (the *midpoint rule*). For Planck 2018 the values of
$1/(aH)$ at $a = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9$ are 8.2, 13.8, 16.2, 16.4 and 15.2
billion years. Their average, $69.8/5 \approx 13.96$, times the step range of 1,
is already within about 1% of the exact result. With 100 steps the estimate is
13.806 billion years.
:::

The Cosmos app evaluates the integral with an adaptive method that refines the
steps automatically until the answer is accurate to about one part in a hundred
million.

## What the age depends on

**The Hubble constant.** For fixed Ω values, $t_0 \propto 1/H_0$. A faster expansion
means a younger universe:

| $H_0$ (km/s/Mpc) | Age for Ωm = 0.31, ΩΛ = 0.69 |
|---|---|
| 67.7 (CMB) | 13.8 billion years |
| 73.0 (Cepheids and supernovae) | 12.8 billion years |

The [[hubble-tension|Hubble tension]] therefore also affects the age.

**Matter and dark energy.** More matter means more deceleration and a younger
universe. Dark energy makes it older.

## Checking the age: the oldest stars

A universe cannot be younger than its oldest stars. Astronomers estimate stellar
ages independently of cosmology:

- **Globular clusters**, dense groups of very old stars in the halo of the Milky Way.
  Comparing their colours and brightnesses with models of stellar evolution gives
  ages of about 12–13.5 billion years.
- **White dwarf cooling.** White dwarfs cool like embers at a predictable rate; the
  faintest ones in old clusters are about 12 billion years old.
- **Radioactive dating.** Uranium and thorium in very old stars decay like a clock
  and give ages of roughly 12–14 billion years, with larger uncertainties.

:::history The age problem
In the 1990s many astronomers believed the universe contained only matter. With
the measured $H_0$ this gave ages of 8–10 billion years, younger than the
globular clusters. The discovery of dark energy in 1998 resolved the conflict: in
ΛCDM the universe is older than its oldest stars, as it must be. The figure above
shows the shaded band of stellar ages crossing the curves.
:::

## Summary

- The age of the universe is $t_0 = \int_0^1 da/(aH)$: the Hubble time times a
  number set by the cosmic contents.
- Empty universe: $1/H_0$. Matter only: $\frac{2}{3}H_0^{-1}$. Flat ΛCDM:
  $0.955\,H_0^{-1} \approx 13.8$ billion years.
- The integral is easy to evaluate numerically; even five steps give about 1%
  accuracy.
- Faster expansion or more matter make the universe younger; dark energy makes it
  older.
- The oldest stars (12–13.5 billion years) are consistent with ΛCDM but not with a
  matter-only universe.
