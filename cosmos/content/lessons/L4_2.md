## Cooling by expansion

The universe is filled with radiation that has a [[blackbody|blackbody]]
spectrum, the kind of light emitted by any object in thermal equilibrium. A
blackbody spectrum is completely described by its temperature $T$; the typical
wavelength of its photons is inversely proportional to it (Wien's law):

$$\lambda_{\mathrm{peak}} \approx \frac{2.9\ \mathrm{mm\,K}}{T}$$

As the universe expands, every photon wavelength stretches by the scale factor.
A stretched blackbody is still a blackbody, just with a lower temperature:

$$T \propto \frac{1}{a} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad T(z) = T_0\,(1+z)$$

with $T_0 = 2.7255$ K today.

:::example Worked example
At recombination, $z \approx 1090$, the temperature was
$T = 2.7255 \times 1091 \approx 2\,970$ K, about half the surface temperature
of the Sun. Back then the "microwave" background glowed orange-red.
:::

The energy density of blackbody radiation grows as the fourth power of the
temperature, $\rho_{\mathrm{r}} c^2 = a_{\mathrm{rad}} T^4$. Since $T \propto a^{-1}$,
this reproduces $\rho_{\mathrm{r}} \propto a^{-4}$ from lesson
[L3.1](lesson:L3.1).

## Temperature as energy

In the early universe it is more natural to measure temperature as a typical
particle energy $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$, where $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ is Boltzmann's constant.
Physicists use the [[electron-volt|electron volt]] (eV):

$$1\ \mathrm{eV} \leftrightarrow 11\,600\ \mathrm{K}, \qquad 1\ \mathrm{MeV} \leftrightarrow 1.16 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{K}$$

:::key Hot enough to create particles
When $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ exceeds a particle's rest energy $mc^2$, collisions between
photons can create particle–antiparticle pairs. An electron has
$mc^2 = 0.511$ MeV, so above about $6 \times 10^{9}$ K the universe was full of
electrons and positrons constantly created and annihilated. As the universe
cooled below each threshold, those particles annihilated and disappeared,
heating the remaining photons slightly.
:::

## Time and temperature in the radiation era

During the radiation era, the Friedmann equation with $\rho \propto T^4$ and
$a \propto t^{1/2}$ gives a simple rule of thumb:

$$T \approx 10^{10}\ \mathrm{K} \times \left(\frac{t}{1\ \mathrm{s}}\right)^{-1/2}$$

- At 1 second: about $10^{10}$ K (1 MeV).
- At 100 seconds: about $10^{9}$ K.
- At 1 hour: about $2 \times 10^{8}$ K.

The exact numbers depend on how many kinds of particles are present, but this
estimate is good enough to follow the story.

## Thermal equilibrium

In the early universe, particles collided so frequently that they shared energy
and stayed in **thermal equilibrium**. A reaction stays in equilibrium as long as
it happens faster than the universe expands, that is, its rate $\Gamma$ exceeds
$H$. When $\Gamma$ drops below $H$, the reaction effectively stops: the particles
**decouple** or **freeze out**.

$$\Gamma \gg H:\ \mathrm{equilibrium} \qquad \Gamma \ll H:\ \mathrm{frozen\ out}$$

This simple criterion explains neutrino decoupling at about 1 second, the
neutron-to-proton ratio in nucleosynthesis, and photon decoupling at
recombination.

## An ocean of photons

Today the CMB contains about **411 photons per cubic centimetre**. By comparison,
there is only about one proton per four cubic metres. The
[[baryon-to-photon-ratio|baryon-to-photon ratio]]

$$\eta = \frac{n_{\mathrm{b}}}{n_\gamma} \approx 6 \times 10^{-10}$$

has stayed constant since the first seconds, because both numbers dilute as
$a^{-3}$. There are more than a billion photons for every proton or neutron. This
tiny number shapes both nucleosynthesis and recombination, as the next lessons
show.

## Summary

- Radiation keeps a blackbody spectrum with $T = T_0(1+z) \propto 1/a$.
- Temperature measures typical particle energy; $1\ \mathrm{MeV} \approx 10^{10}$ K.
- In the radiation era, $T \approx 10^{10}\,\mathrm{K}\,(t/\mathrm{s})^{-1/2}$.
- Reactions freeze out when their rate falls below the expansion rate.
- There are about $1.6 \times 10^{9}$ photons per baryon.
